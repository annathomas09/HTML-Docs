13.1

Scope & Relationship to Document 3

This document covers everything outside the building envelope Document 3 defines: frontyard, backyard, and the boundary buffer running the full perimeter. It does not touch the semi-outdoor Right Extension loggia or the Main Hall's indoor pond — both are Document 3's, already resolved in §3.4 and §3.6.

Document 3 §3.7 set a ~15,000 sq ft target plot (mid-covers scenario) against a ~6,000 sq ft built-up footprint. That leaves roughly 9,000 sq ft of actual landscape to design — a working range of 7,900–10,150 sq ft across the low–high covers scenarios. Everything below assumes the mid figure and scales the same way Document 3's sizing table does.

This is the first of four new environmental documents. Document 14 (Water Harvesting), Document 15 (Green Energy), and Document 16 (Indoor Planting) all build on the zones this document lays out.

13.2

Design Philosophy

Document 0 §0.3 locks water-paired-with-greenery as the one non-negotiable brand signature, expressed indoors through Document 3's ponds and ring-pendant fixtures. Outdoors, the same DNA reads differently: layered and naturalistic rather than manicured-formal, native-first with ornamental accents carrying color and fragrance, and — because this is a working kitchen's backyard as much as a guest's garden — genuinely useful in places, not purely decorative.

Core — Fixed Everywhere

Every planted zone mixes native species with ornamental ones, per the principle Document 3 §3.7 already set for the boundary: native planting supports local pollinators and holds up on real Delhi water and soil conditions; ornamental planting carries the seasonal color and fragrance a hospitality flagship needs. Neither substitutes for the other.

13.4

Frontyard

The arrival sequence, and the first brand impression before a guest ever reaches the glass facade Document 3 §3.4 built specifically to "announce itself from the street." Landscape's job here is to frame that moment, not compete with it: flowering trees and taller plantings sit off-axis, to the sides of the direct approach, leaving the facade's sightline from the street genuinely clear. A single stone path — same material language as the Main Hall's walkway, per Document 3 §3.4 — leads straight from the street edge to the entrance, wide enough for two people walking side by side.

Low, layered planting borders the path itself: groundcover and low ornamentals close to the walking surface, taller flowering shrubs and one or two canopy trees set back near the boundary line, where they contribute to the perimeter ring described in §13.6 rather than crowding the entrance.

13.5

Backyard

Document 3 §3.4's Back Extension is split into two moods — Jaali + Drape Lounge and Lantern + Rose Terrace — and the terrace half looks directly out over this space. The backyard is designed to reward that view: rose beds facing the terrace, underplanted with lower seasonal color so the terrace's sightline reads as a garden at every time of year, not a bare bed between bloom cycles.

Because Document 6 §6.5 already positions the Back Extension for evening social use, backyard planting near the terrace leans toward night-fragrant species — the kind of garden that becomes more present after dark, not less. §13.7's plant palette calls these out specifically.

Practical — Kitchen Garden

The backyard's far corner sits directly against the Kitchen block Document 3 §3.4 placed in the site plan's top-right corner. That adjacency is deliberate: a small kitchen garden — curry leaf, mint, coriander, lemongrass, tulsi — sits at that corner, close enough for kitchen staff to actually use, not just visible from a dining table. It's a working garden first, a pretty one second.

13.7

Seasonal Notes

Delhi's calendar is unforgiving on planting choices that assume one climate year-round. Amaltas and Gulmohar carry May–June, the hottest and least outdoor-friendly stretch — which is deliberately when the strolling and terrace zones need the most visual reward for the least likely footfall. Roses and the fragrant night-bloomers carry October through February, when Back Extension's evening terrace use is at its most comfortable and most likely to be full. Monsoon (July–September) favors the native canopy trees and groundcover, which tolerate the water load better than the ornamental flowering trees do.

Irrigation demand shifts hard across these same seasons — heavy monsoon runoff to manage, real drought stress by May. That system is Document 14's to design, not this one's; this document only fixes which plants need to be kept alive.
