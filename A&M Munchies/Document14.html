14.1

Scope

Two separate systems, kept deliberately apart: rainwater harvesting (roof and hardscape runoff, clean enough to store and, if needed, top up the ornamental water features Document 3 §3.6 describes) and greywater reuse (washroom handwash only — scoped conservatively, for reasons §14.4 explains). Both ultimately feed Document 13's irrigation demand; neither is designed here to the engineering level Document 12 already declined to own for kitchen systems — pump sizing, pipe runs, and filtration-media specifics are an MEP consultant's task once a real site exists.

Kitchen wastewater (pot-wash, prep-sink runoff) is explicitly not in scope for reuse here — see §14.4.

14.2

Design Philosophy

Document 0 §0.3 makes water paired with greenery the one fixed brand signature. This document is where that signature earns its water responsibly rather than drawing it purely from the municipal supply — Delhi's groundwater table has been falling for decades, and a flagship built around visible water features has a real obligation to not simply be a net drain on it.

Core — Fixed Everywhere

Harvested rainwater may top up the ornamental ponds Document 3 §3.6 describes. Greywater never does — it's irrigation-only, full stop. This isn't a cost-saving shortcut; it's the difference between water guests dine next to and water that's had soap and handwashing residue in it.

14.4

Greywater — Scope & What's Deliberately Excluded

Washroom handwash basins only. Kitchen wastewater — pot-wash, prep-sink runoff, anything that's touched food — is excluded from this system entirely, and stays on its own grease-trap-to-sewer path, which is Document 12's and Document 9's territory, not this document's. Reusing kitchen greywater for irrigation is technically possible but needs food-grade-adjacent filtration to avoid clogging drip lines and attracting pests; at this planning stage, that's a real complexity this document isn't going to wave away with an optimistic number.

Rough planning estimate: 400–600 L/day from washroom handwash across customer and staff use at the mid-covers scenario. Treat as a placeholder — real fixture counts come from the architect's washroom layout, not this document.

14.6

Regulatory Context

Rainwater harvesting has been mandatory in Delhi for plots of 100 sq m and above since 2001, enforced by the Delhi Jal Board. The mandate has seen renewed enforcement in 2026 — the "Catch the Rain 2026" campaign adds a rebate of up to 10% on water bills and financial assistance of up to ₹50,000 for compliant installations, while a separate NGT-driven push directs MCD to withhold construction NOCs on plots above 100 sq m without a functioning system.

Compliance — Not Optional

At the mid-covers target of ~1,398 sq m (Document 3 §3.7), this plot falls in the 501–2,000 sq m penalty band. Non-residential non-compliance in that band runs to ₹1.5 lakh (the residential ₹1 lakh rate plus the 50% non-residential loading) — not a rounding error, and a real reason this system gets designed now rather than retrofitted later. This isn't this document inventing a requirement; it's already load-bearing for Document 9's licensing work.

Sourced from Delhi Jal Board public guidance and 2026 reporting on the Catch the Rain campaign and NGT enforcement directive — worth an independent legal check against DJB's current guidelines before this becomes a cost-estimation line item in Document 10.
