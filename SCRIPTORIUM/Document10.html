01 · The Governing Principle

What the outdoor zone is for

The backyard of SCRIPTORIUM is not decorative. It earns its area three ways simultaneously: it provides the passive evaporative cooling that reduces the café's mechanical cooling load in Delhi's peak summer months (April–June); it gives the café room to expand its seating capacity during rush hours without compromising the experience for visitors who are there to stroll or sit quietly; and it is the ecological argument of the campus — a native-planted, deep-water pond environment that demonstrates that a commercial building in South Delhi can support pollinators, birds, and aquatic life without requiring a rural setting.

These three functions must coexist without conflict. The café overflow zone, the strolling circuit, and the pond must each have their own clearly defined territory — not because they cannot be adjacent, but because a café chair placed in the middle of a strolling path is neither good seating nor a good path. The spatial budget in Section 02 resolves this by assigning each function its own footprint with clear edges and transitions.

The governing principle
The outdoor zone serves the building's ecology, the café's overflow, and the visitor's capacity to breathe — all three, at the same time, without any one crowding out the others.
In practical terms: at peak capacity on a busy Saturday, every café overflow chair is occupied, the strolling path is in use by visitors moving between zones, and the pond edge is accessible for someone who wants to stand at the water and look at the fish. None of these activities interrupts the others because the spatial budget has separated them by design, not by hope.

02a · Energy & Rooftop Strategy

SmartFlower — replacing rooftop solar · Rooftop pollinator garden

Two decisions in this section depart from the Architecture Brief (Document 02), which specified flat solar panels on the wing roofs behind the colonnade parapet. Both departures are justified, and both are improvements — on aesthetic grounds, on maintenance grounds, and on ecological grounds. They are documented here with full reasoning so the architect and structural engineer can incorporate them from the design stage.

These decisions affect Document 02 (Architecture Brief) and Document 03 (Cost Estimation). The flat rooftop solar panels are removed from the energy specification. A cluster of 4–6 SmartFlower+ units replaces them as the campus's only on-site generation source — covering an estimated 11–27% of total demand — positioned in the new frontyard extension zone (Section 07a). The flat wing roofs are repurposed as a pollinator garden, which changes the structural load requirement on those roofs. Both changes are costed in the cross-document impact table (Section 10). The architect must be notified of both decisions at the first design meeting — the roof structural load change in particular must be designed in from the foundation, not retrofitted.

SmartFlower — the case for it

Your objections to flat rooftop solar panels are all technically valid and individually documented by the SmartFlower's own comparison data. In order:

Aesthetics next to the library tower. The architecture brief specifies a Gothic collegiate library tower rising to approximately 24–26m — the most dramatic and identity-defining element of the building. Flat photovoltaic panels on the wing roofs beside it create exactly the visual conflict you described: a heritage-register building with industrial-grade rooftop hardware bolted onto it. The colonnade parapet was specifically designed in Document 02 to hide those panels from street level, which itself signals that they were always understood as aesthetically problematic. Removing them entirely and replacing them with a small cluster of sculptural ground-mounted SmartFlower units in the frontyard resolves the conflict at the source rather than managing it with a parapet.

Dust and water waste. Delhi's air quality means that flat solar panels without active cleaning lose significant output to dust accumulation — an estimated 5% output loss from sediment alone, and in Delhi's specific pollution conditions, potentially more. Cleaning flat panels requires water, staff time, and access equipment. The SmartFlower's self-cleaning system — long brushes on the underside of each petal that sweep the panel surface every time the flower opens and closes — means it cleans itself automatically every morning and evening with no water, no staff time, and no cleaning equipment. This is not a marginal convenience; it is a meaningful maintenance cost reduction in a city where rooftop dust accumulation is a daily reality.

Energy performance. The SmartFlower's dual-axis sun tracking means it follows the sun from east to west and adjusts its vertical angle continuously, keeping the panels at the optimal 90° angle to the sun all day. This produces up to 40% more energy than a fixed flat panel of the same nominal capacity. Each SmartFlower (2.31–2.5 kW nominal) effectively delivers the equivalent output of a 4 kW conventional fixed system, and realistically produces 5,500–6,200 kWh/year in Delhi's irradiance conditions. The frontyard extension (Section 07a) now provides room for 4–6 units rather than the single unit this section originally specified — a combined 22,000–37,200 kWh/year, covering an estimated 11–27% of the campus's total electricity demand depending on final load calculations. That is a meaningful, honest contribution, not the campus's sole or primary electricity source — grid supply remains the backstop for the remainder. What the SmartFlower cluster does uniquely is put the campus's only on-site generation hardware on display, front and centre, as a visible statement rather than a hidden rooftop system.

Uniqueness in India. Pricing and product listings for SmartFlower systems exist on Indian platforms as of 2026, and at least one Indian manufacturer (Souleet Makers LLP, Pune) offers a locally adapted solar flower variant. However, a cluster of SmartFlowers deployed as a deliberate frontyard architectural element at a commercial campus of SCRIPTORIUM's identity — not tucked behind a building but positioned as a visual anchor of the entry garden — has not been done in India at this scale. This is the correct context for it. SCRIPTORIUM's frontyard already has Pride of India trees and a carved sandstone signage block making a statement about what the campus is. A cluster of SmartFlowers in the new extension zone of that same frontyard, visible from the street, opening and closing with the sun, is a living demonstration of the same values the building articulates in stone and glass. One correction for the record: SmartFlower is an Austrian-engineered design (smartflower GmbH), not a Boston-based product — an earlier note in this document's glossary had this wrong, and it has been corrected there too.

Rooftop pollinator garden — the flat wing roofs repurposed

With the SmartFlower in the frontyard handling solar generation, the flat wing roofs behind the colonnade parapet (as specified in Document 02) are freed from their solar panel role. The proposal here is to convert them into a rooftop pollinator garden — a planted green roof that is private (not publicly accessible), ecologically functional, and visually invisible from street level (screened by the colonnade parapet, as the solar panels were).

The rooftop pollinator garden does the things that a flat roof cannot do and that the ground-level garden cannot do alone: it creates a continuous ecological corridor at height — connecting the campus to the tree canopy of neighbouring plots and the wider urban ecosystem. It extends the campus's pollinator habitat vertically. And it plants the species whose flower colours — vivid, contrasting, and deliberately outside the warm-neutral ground-level palette — would read as discordant in the frontyard or backyard but are correct and beautiful in an elevated, visually separated space visible primarily to birds and insects.

Structural load requirement — the design change that must happen first

A conventional flat roof in Indian commercial construction is designed for a live load of approximately 1.5–2.0 kN/m² (roughly 150–200 kg/m²) — enough for maintenance access but not for sustained planting. A planted green roof with growing medium, plants, drainage layer, and occasional pooled water requires a structural live load of 3.0–5.0 kN/m² (300–500 kg/m²) for a shallow extensive green roof (growing medium 100–150mm deep), or up to 7.0–10.0 kN/m² for a deeper intensive roof garden. For the pollinator garden specified here — a shallow extensive green roof, 100–150mm growing medium, lightweight structural growing medium (not soil), planted with meadow-style low perennials and bulbs — the structural live load requirement is approximately 3.5–4.5 kN/m². This must be designed into the roof structure from the foundation. Retrofitting a green roof onto a standard-specification flat roof is not feasible — the additional load is too great for an unmodified slab. The architect must specify the enhanced roof structure in the structural drawings before construction begins. This is manageable and common in commercial green-roof projects — it is a design decision, not a construction challenge.

The ground-level colour palette (Section 08b) is warm-neutral: sandstone buff, brass gold, dark green, parchment cream. Target pollinators, nesting habitat, and monitoring — informed by the pollinator garden feasibility report

A commissioned feasibility report on the rooftop pollinator garden adds useful detail on who the garden is actually for and what they need beyond flowers. Delhi's rooftop pollinator community is realistically bees, butterflies, hoverflies, and — as the Salvia row above already notes — sunbirds. On the bee side, expect the two common Indian honeybees (Apis cerana, the Indian hive bee, and Apis dorsata, the giant rock bee) alongside native solitary bees such as carpenter bees (Xylocopa) and mason bees (Osmia). On the butterfly side, expect generalist urban species such as the Plain Tiger (Danaus chrysippus) and Common Mormon (Papilio polytes).

The detail worth acting on: roughly 70% of native bee species nest in bare or lightly mulched ground rather than in hives, and the remainder nest in hollow cavities. A wall-to-wall planted roof with no bare patches quietly excludes most of its potential bee visitors. The fix is simple and low-cost — leave a few small patches of bare or lightly mulched substrate between plant clusters (rather than covering every inch with Sedum or mulch), and tuck one or two bundles of hollow bamboo or reed stems into a sheltered corner of the roof for cavity-nesting species. Leaving dead stems and seed heads standing over winter (already noted for Echinacea above) does double duty as bird food and overwintering shelter for insects — extend that same standing-through-winter approach to the Salvia and Gaillardia stems rather than cutting the bed back hard in autumn. Pesticides are not used anywhere on this roof, in keeping with the rest of the campus's planting philosophy.

For tracking whether the garden is actually working, two low-effort metrics are worth adopting: a seasonal visual estimate of live vegetation cover, targeting 80%+ cover within the first one to two growing seasons, and a quarterly five-minute pollinator count on a fixed spot on the roof (same time of day, same season each year) simply logging how many bees and butterflies are seen and of how many kinds. Neither requires specialist expertise — the facilities team can do both as part of the existing quarterly inspection already in the maintenance calendar (Section 07) — and together they give an honest, low-cost answer to "is this actually attracting pollinators" beyond just "the plants are alive."

Irrigation on the rooftop: The pollinator garden requires supplementary irrigation during the dry summer months (March–June) before the monsoon arrives. A simple drip irrigation system (timer-controlled, fed mainly from the building's general water supply, with harvested rainwater overflow as a secondary top-up — see Section 04, Step 5) supplies water directly to the growing medium. Once established (Year 2 onward), the Sedum, Gaillardia, Salvia, and Lavender are all genuinely drought-tolerant and survive without irrigation during mild dry spells — only during peak Delhi summer (May–June) does irrigation become essential. The monsoon (July–September) provides all the water the roof garden needs for three months with no supplementation. Estimated irrigation requirement: approximately 2–4 litres/m² per day in peak summer. For 100 sq m of roof garden, that is 200–400 litres/day in May–June only. Water-efficient drip delivery means the actual consumption is at the lower end of this range.

On the combined effect of SmartFlower + rooftop garden: SCRIPTORIUM now has a complete vertical ecological stack — pollinators at ground level (the Kaner, Tulsi, Champa, Marigold, Porterweed, and pond marginals), pollinators at roof level (the wildflower meadow), and the birds and insects connecting these two levels through the vertical airspace of the campus. The SmartFlower in the frontyard opens every morning and closes every evening, tracking the sun — a living mechanical object that mirrors the biological movement of the plants around it. The combination of a biomimetic solar sculpture in the frontyard and a rooftop wildflower meadow above is not a marketing gesture — it is a coherent ecological and architectural argument about what a campus like this should be.

03 · Pond Design & Engineering

The koi and goldfish pond

The pond serves four functions: ecological (supporting aquatic life, native marginal plants, pollinators, and birds); thermal (evaporative cooling of the café via the SW–NE breeze path established in the Architecture Brief); visual (the primary outdoor landscape feature, visible from the café through the full-height glass wall); and regulatory (it functions as the campus's mandatory rainwater harvesting recharge structure under the Delhi Jal Board's RWH mandate for plots over 100 sq m). These four functions together justify a pond that is substantially larger and deeper than a conventional ornamental water feature.

Why depth is the non-negotiable specification

Delhi's peak summer air temperatures reach 44–46°C in May and June. Water surface temperature in a shallow ornamental pond (less than 0.6m deep) in full Delhi summer sun can reach 34–38°C — lethal for koi and very stressful for goldfish. A pond with shallow water also loses volume rapidly to evaporation during the dry summer months (April–June), before the monsoon arrives. The minimum safe depth for koi overwinter (Delhi winters drop to 3–6°C) and oversummer (Delhi summers reach 44°C) is 1.2m. The design specification here is 1.5m minimum at the deepest section, with a graded profile that slopes from a marginal shelf at 0.3m to the full 1.5m at the centre. This profile serves the ecology: different aquatic plants grow at different depths, and the deeper cool zone gives fish a thermal refuge in summer.

Pond construction specification

Material correction — why granite replaces both Dholpur sandstone and Kota stone in this pond. An earlier draft of this section specified Dholpur sandstone for the exterior coping and Kota stone for the interior wet surfaces. Both were wrong, for the same underlying reason: both are porous sedimentary stones, and porous sedimentary stone in permanent water contact can leach minerals and gradually shift pond pH — the same property that makes limestone unsuitable is present, to varying degrees, in both sandstone and Kota stone (itself a type of limestone). Multiple independent pond-construction sources converge on the same correction: granite, being igneous and chemically inert, is the standard safe material for fish pond construction, while sandstone and limestone are explicitly flagged as materials experienced pond builders avoid. Granite also solves the visual-harmony concern raised separately — using one consistent stone family throughout (rather than two visually distinct geological materials, which can read as two unrelated projects stitched together) avoids the disjointed "split-screen" look that mixing a flat sedimentary slab waterfall with a different rugged igneous pond base can produce. The two granite varieties specified below were chosen specifically to preserve the building's existing colour language — Antique Beige Granite echoes Dholpur's warm tone for the coping, and Steel Grey Granite echoes the brand's own "Kota Grey" accent colour for the interior, so nothing about the campus's established palette needs to change, only the literal stone type.

Fish hiding structures — built into the rock construction Integrated stone caves, not floating plant cover alone. 04 · Water Supply System

Filling and maintaining the pond — the hybrid system

Three constraints define the water supply strategy for the SCRIPTORIUM pond, all established in earlier documents:

  • No borewell. Delhi NCR groundwater is classified as over-exploited by the CGWA. Borewell NOCs for ornamental/non-essential uses are explicitly withheld in this classification. A borewell dug without a NOC is an illegal extraction and carries substantial penalties (Document 01).
  • Rainwater harvesting is mandatory but cannot be the sole supply. Delhi receives approximately 650–750mm of annual rainfall, concentrated almost entirely in the monsoon (July–September). The four dry months before the monsoon (March–June) are also the months of peak evaporation — a 35,000-litre pond loses approximately 800–1,200 litres per day to evaporation in May and June. Relying solely on rainwater means the pond drops significantly in the months when it is most visible from the café.
  • The pond must function as the campus's RWH recharge structure to satisfy the Delhi Jal Board's mandatory rainwater harvesting requirement for plots over 100 sq m (Document 01).

The solution is a hybrid system with three inputs and one overflow output, designed so that rainwater is always the primary and preferred source, municipal supply is the top-up when needed, and the overflow is managed rather than wasted.

1
Rooftop Rainwater Harvesting — Primary Input
The campus roof (flat wing roofs, approximately 3,500–4,000 sq ft catchment area excluding the library's Gothic lantern) is connected via downpipes to a first-flush diverter. The first-flush diverter discards the first 20–30 litres of water from each rainfall event (which carries the highest concentration of dust, bird droppings, and accumulated particulate from Delhi's air) and then routes the cleaner subsequent flow to the system. From the first-flush diverter, water passes through a coarse mesh filter and then into the pond via a submerged inlet pipe (submerged to avoid disturbing fish and to prevent aeration of the incoming water, which can introduce oxygen faster than the fish can handle). Delhi's monsoon months (July–September) provide enough rainfall to fill the pond and maintain it above the minimum level for three to four months. This is the regulatory compliance component — the DJB RWH verification requires this system to be installed and functional before the construction NOC is issued.
2
Municipal Water (DJB Supply) — Supplementary Top-Up
A dedicated 2,000-litre underground sump tank, separate from the building's main domestic water storage, is connected to the Delhi Jal Board municipal supply line. This tank feeds the pond via a float-valve controlled inlet — the same mechanism as a toilet cistern. When the pond water level drops below a set point (triggered by evaporation in summer), the float valve opens and draws from the sump tank. The sump tank is topped up from the DJB supply line automatically.

Treatment mechanism inside the sump tank — full specification: Delhi's municipal supply contains chlorine (0.2–0.5 mg/L typically) and sometimes chloramine (chlorine + ammonia compound, used in some Delhi zones as a more stable disinfectant). Both are lethal to fish at pond concentrations. The treatment system has three components installed inside or on the sump tank:

(1) Sodium thiosulphate dosing unit — a gravity-fed drip dosing container mounted above the sump inlet pipe. A solution of sodium thiosulphate (Na₂S₂O₃, food-grade, available from aquarium suppliers) drips at a calibrated rate into the incoming DJB supply water as it fills the sump. Sodium thiosulphate neutralises chlorine within seconds — the reaction is immediate and the products are harmless. The dosing rate: approximately 1 gram of sodium thiosulphate per 200 litres of water neutralises 1 ppm of chlorine. With a 2,000-litre tank and DJB chlorine at 0.5 ppm, approximately 5 grams per fill. The dosing container is refilled monthly — a 2-minute maintenance task.

(2) Activated carbon filter on the sump outlet pipe — a 10-inch filter housing with a granular activated carbon (GAC) cartridge installed on the pipe between the sump tank and the pond inlet. Activated carbon adsorbs chloramine (which sodium thiosulphate alone does not fully neutralise), residual organic compounds, and any taste/odour compounds in the municipal supply. Replace cartridge every 3 months or after 10,000 litres throughput (whichever comes first). Budget ₹400–800 per cartridge replacement — negligible cost.

(3) 30-minute holding time before pond entry — the float valve on the pond side is set to draw from the sump only after a minimum of 30 minutes has elapsed since the sump last filled. This holding time allows: (a) any residual chlorine to off-gas from the water surface (chlorine is volatile and dissipates within 20–30 minutes of aeration in a non-sealed container); (b) the water to equilibrate to ambient temperature (cold water from the supply pipe can shock fish if introduced too rapidly). The 30-minute hold is built into the float valve timing mechanism — a simple electronic timer relay or a slow-flow restriction orifice on the outlet pipe achieves this without additional hardware.

A floating pond thermometer and a basic water test kit (pH, ammonia, nitrite, nitrate, chlorine) are used monthly to verify water quality. If chlorine tests above 0.05 ppm in the pond itself at any point, increase the sodium thiosulphate dose immediately and check the carbon filter cartridge.
3
Greywater from A&M Munchies — Explored, Not Primary
The café's sink rinse water (from the barista station rinse sink and the prep sink — not the dishwasher waste, not the cooking drain, not any water that has contacted detergent or food grease) is the one greywater stream clean enough to be useful after basic filtration. Rather than directing this to the pond (where even trace food organics would add to the biological load and risk algae spikes), it is directed after filtration to groundwater recharge via an expanded soakaway system. The reasoning: CGWA has classified Delhi NCR groundwater as over-exploited. Every litre that percolates into the ground rather than entering the storm drain is a meaningful contribution to the groundwater table. The café generates approximately 50–80 litres of clean rinse water per operating day — over a year, 18,000–29,000 litres of recharge potential that would otherwise be wasted to the drain.

The greywater recharge system: The rinse sink's drain connects (via a dedicated pipe separate from the general kitchen drain) to a two-stage treatment unit before the soakaway: (1) a grease trap (small, 20-litre capacity — catches any incidental oil before it can seal the soakaway); (2) a coarse sand filter chamber (300mm × 300mm × 500mm, filled with coarse sand and gravel, easily cleaned quarterly). From the filter, the water enters the soakaway pit. This system is separate from the main soakaway (which handles pond overflow and filter backwash) — a secondary soakaway pit at the west side of the plot, adjacent to the boundary planting, where it simultaneously irrigates the roots of the boundary hedge. Install the two-stage treatment unit during fit-out at the time of café kitchen plumbing — retrofitting it later is significantly more expensive. Cost: ₹15,000–30,000 for the grease trap, sand filter chamber, and connecting pipework. This is a one-time cost with zero ongoing operating cost beyond quarterly filter cleaning.
4
Overflow Management — Monsoon Excess
During peak monsoon (July–August), the pond will receive more water than it loses to evaporation. The overflow outlet is set at the designed operating water level — 100mm below the coping stone — and connects via a buried pipe to a soakaway pit at the rear of the plot, adjacent to the boundary planting. The soakaway pit (1m × 1m × 1.5m deep, filled with rubble and gravel) allows excess water to percolate into the soil slowly, recharging the groundwater table rather than running off to the drain. The soakaway also receives the filter backwash water. This completes the water cycle: rain in → pond → overflow → soil recharge → groundwater. A system that is genuinely circular, not just a claim.
5
Rooftop Garden Cross-Link — Overflow Now Splits Two Ways (Addition)
Steps 1–4 above are unchanged from the prior revision of this document. This step is new, and connects the pond system to the rooftop pollinator garden's irrigation (Section 02a) rather than altering anything already specified. The harvested rooftop rainwater described in Step 1, on its way down from the wing-roof catchment, now passes through a simple tee-and-valve diverter before it reaches the first-flush diverter and pond inlet. This splits the collected water into two paths: the majority continues down the existing downpipe route to the pond exactly as Step 1 describes, unchanged; a smaller portion is diverted into a modest roof-level buffer tank (500–1,000 litres — small, since the garden's need is far lower than the pond's) that feeds the rooftop garden's drip irrigation lines already specified in Section 02a. Once that buffer tank is full, any further water simply continues on to the pond, so the pond's supply is not meaningfully reduced by this addition.

This does not make rainwater the rooftop garden's main water source. Delhi's rainfall is monsoon-concentrated (the same constraint that opens this section), so the roof buffer tank is realistically only useful from the monsoon through the following one or two months — a seasonal bonus, not a year-round supply. For the peak dry irrigation demand (March–June, already quantified in Section 02a at 200–400 litres/day for 100 sq m of roof garden), the drip irrigation system draws mainly from the building's general water supply, plumbed to the roof the same way any other floor's water point would be. The practical division of labour: general supply covers the garden reliably, every day, all year; diverted rainwater overflow tops up that same buffer tank opportunistically after monsoon rain, reducing how much general supply the garden draws down in the shoulder months without ever being relied upon to carry it alone.

Water volume calculation: A 500 sq ft (46 sq m) surface area pond averaging 1.1m depth = approximately 50,600 litres (50.6 cubic metres) of water. Delhi peak summer evaporation rate for an open water surface: approximately 8–12mm per day. At 10mm/day over a 500 sq ft surface = approximately 466 litres per day of top-up required in peak summer. The 2,000-litre sump tank with daily DJB replenishment comfortably covers this. On a day with no DJB supply (which does occur in Delhi), the pond drops by approximately 10mm — imperceptible visually and not harmful to fish over a single day. The system is resilient to intermittent supply.

The fish introduction protocol: The pond must complete its nitrogen cycle before fish are introduced — minimum 4–6 weeks of running the filtration system on fishless cycling (adding ammonia manually to seed the beneficial bacteria that convert ammonia → nitrite → nitrate). Introducing koi or goldfish into a pond that has not cycled will result in rapid fish death from ammonia toxicity. This is not a design issue — it is an operational protocol that must be written into the campus opening schedule. The pond goes live at least 6 weeks before fish are introduced, and water tests must confirm: ammonia 0 ppm, nitrite 0 ppm, nitrate below 40 ppm, pH 7.0–8.0.

Fish stocking specification

On crystal-clear water — the four-system approach: Clear pond water in Delhi's climate requires mechanical filtration (bottom drain → filter chamber → return), UV sterilisation (kills suspended algae), biological filtration (beneficial bacteria converting ammonia → nitrite → nitrate), and Karp carp as bottom feeders consuming detritus before it clouds the water. Together in a properly cycled pond these produce water clear enough to see the Kota stone floor at 1.5m depth.

On fish size, growth, and the pond's long-term carrying capacity: Koi grow throughout their lifetime. In the right conditions — clean water, correct temperature, adequate space — a Kohaku Koi grows approximately 5–8cm per year and reaches 60–80cm in 8–10 years. At full adult size, each large Koi requires 1,000–2,000 litres of territory. The SCRIPTORIUM pond at ~50,000 litres can comfortably support 10 large adult Koi alongside goldfish and Karp — but these fish are introduced as 15–20cm juveniles and grow into that capacity over a decade. The pond is never stocked beyond 50% of its adult carrying capacity at time of introduction. If any fish outgrows the pond, the correct response is rehoming to a larger private pond. The campus pond logbook records each fish's approximate length at each annual service so the specialist can project when the pond approaches its limit. The rear planting buffer deliberately preserves a 200 sq ft expansion zone — if needed, the pond can be enlarged to ~750–800 sq ft surface area (~70,000 litres) without disrupting any other outdoor zone. Budget approximately ₹4–7L for such expansion at Year 5.

05 · Native Planting Strategy

Plants that earn their place

Every plant in the SCRIPTORIUM landscape earns its presence by serving at least one ecological function: providing food or nesting habitat for pollinators or birds, improving water quality in or around the pond, providing shade that reduces the thermal load on the building or the pond, supporting soil health through deep roots, or acting as a wind buffer that slows the summer loo (hot dry wind) before it reaches the outdoor seating. No plant is here purely for appearance — though many of the species chosen are also beautiful.

On the principle of native planting: Native species are those that evolved in the Indo-Gangetic plain and the Delhi region specifically. They are adapted to Delhi's extreme climate (44°C summers, 3°C winters, 700mm concentrated monsoon rainfall, 8–10 months of drought between rainy seasons), require no supplementary irrigation once established, and support the local food web in ways that exotic ornamental plants cannot. A Bougainvillea looks spectacular but supports virtually no native insect species. An Indian Wild Fig supports 70+ species of insects, birds, and small mammals. The planting strategy prioritises native species in every category where a native species exists and is appropriate.

The outdoor overflow zone is not a permanent extension of the café. 07a · The Frontyard

The entry garden — what the street sees

The frontyard is the campus's first argument. A visitor approaching SCRIPTORIUM from the Saket Metro or from Said-ul-Ajaib village sees the Dholpur sandstone facade with the carved wordmark before they see anything inside. Per the updated Architecture Brief, the frontyard now runs to roughly 15m total depth, up from the original 4–5m, so that it can hold the 4–6 SmartFlower+ units specified in Section 02a. It reads as two zones in sequence: the entry garden below — the original 4–5m zone between (what is now an inner) boundary and the building entrance — is carried over from this document's prior revision exactly as it was, with no tree, path, or planting bed repositioned; a new ~10m extension zone, described further down this section, is appended beyond it, toward the street. The frontyard as a whole must still do the three things it always has: signal clearly that this is a place of quality; provide a planted buffer that reduces the noise and dust of the road before it reaches the building entrance; and welcome visitors of every kind without being either intimidating or generic.

What the frontyard is not: It is not a parking forecourt. It is not a concrete apron. It is not a row of potted plastic plants. A campus that has spent ₹20–28 crore on its building does not greet its visitors with a bare cement entry. The frontyard is planted, paved in permeable sandstone sett, and defined by two Pride of India trees that announce the campus from the street whether or not they are in flower.

08a · Indoor Plants & Planters

Replacing the air purifier — plants by zone

SCRIPTORIUM has no mechanical air purifier. Instead, each zone of the building uses a curated selection of indoor plants that perform documented air purification functions — absorbing VOCs (volatile organic compounds from furniture, flooring adhesives, and paint), increasing humidity slightly (beneficial in Delhi's extreme dry season), and absorbing CO₂ during daylight hours. The selection for each zone is governed by two constraints: the light conditions available in that zone (the library has different light from the stationery floors), and the brand identity (planters must follow the zone's colour palette without creating a jungle aesthetic that distracts from the architecture).

The governing principle for indoor planting
One well-chosen plant in the right planter in the right position is better than twelve plants scattered without intention.
The risk with indoor planting in a commercial space is overcrowding — too many plants, too many planter styles, too many sizes, creates visual noise that competes with the space itself. SCRIPTORIUM's indoor planting is edited: a maximum of 2–3 species per zone, in a consistent planter family, positioned where they are structurally useful (at the base of columns, on shelf ends, at room corners) not decoratively scattered.

Water & Pond Engineering
EPDM LinerEthylene Propylene Diene Monomer
A synthetic rubber membrane used to waterproof ponds. Unlike concrete (which cracks) or PVC (which degrades in UV and becomes brittle), EPDM remains flexible across the full temperature range Delhi produces — from 3°C in winter to 60°C on a sun-exposed surface in May. Fish-safe grade EPDM contains no plasticisers or leaching chemicals that harm aquatic life. A correctly installed EPDM liner lasts 25–30 years.
First-Flush Diverter
A device installed on the downpipe from a roof that automatically discards the first 20–30 litres of water from each rainfall event. The first flush of rain carries the highest concentration of pollutants — dust, bird droppings, atmospheric particles — that have accumulated on the roof surface since the last rain. Diverting this first flush prevents these pollutants from entering the pond or the rainwater storage. After the first flush, the cleaner subsequent rainwater is directed to the pond or tank. Mandatory in any rainwater harvesting system that feeds a fish pond.
UV SteriliserUltraviolet Steriliser
A device installed inline on the pond's return pipe (after mechanical filtration, before the water re-enters the pond) that passes water through a chamber containing a UV-C light bulb. UV-C light at the correct intensity damages the DNA of single-celled organisms — suspended algae (which causes green water), harmful bacteria, and parasites — killing them before they can reproduce. The result is clear, biologically cleaner water. The UV bulb must be replaced annually regardless of apparent function — its effectiveness degrades long before it stops glowing.
Nitrogen Cycle
The biological process by which the beneficial bacteria in a pond filter convert toxic ammonia (produced by fish waste and decomposing organic matter) first into nitrite (also toxic), then into nitrate (far less toxic, removed by water changes and plant uptake). A new pond must complete this cycle — which takes 4–6 weeks — before fish can be introduced safely. Introducing fish into an uncycled pond exposes them to ammonia and nitrite poisoning, which is rapidly fatal. The cycle is confirmed by testing: ammonia = 0 ppm, nitrite = 0 ppm.
Bottom Drain
A gravity-fed drain outlet at the lowest point of the pond floor that continuously draws settled detritus (fish waste, uneaten food, leaf debris) from the pond bottom to the external mechanical filter, where it is removed from the water before the water is returned to the pond. Without a bottom drain, detritus accumulates on the pond floor, decomposes, and releases ammonia — the primary cause of poor water quality in garden ponds. A bottom drain is not optional in a fish pond of this size and stocking level.
Soakaway
A subsurface drainage structure — a pit filled with rubble and gravel — that receives excess water (pond overflow in monsoon, filter backwash water) and allows it to percolate slowly into the surrounding soil, recharging the groundwater table rather than running off to the surface drain. A soakaway does not store water permanently — it disperses it. For a plot in Delhi NCR where groundwater is over-exploited (CGWA designation), every litre that soaks back into the ground rather than running to the storm drain is a small contribution to the groundwater recharge the region needs.

Planting & Ecology
Native Species
A plant species that evolved naturally in a specific geographic region without human introduction — adapted over millennia to the local climate, soil, rainfall pattern, and biological community. Native species in the context of this document means species native to the Indo-Gangetic plain and the Delhi region specifically. Native plants require no supplementary irrigation once established, support the local food web (insects, birds, pollinators) in ways exotic species cannot, and are inherently resilient to Delhi's climate extremes. The preference for native species throughout this document is not aesthetic — it is ecological and practical.
VOCVolatile Organic Compound
Organic chemicals that evaporate readily at room temperature, releasing gases into the indoor air. Sources in a building like SCRIPTORIUM include: formaldehyde (from new book bindings, MDF furniture, adhesives), benzene (from paints, cleaning products), xylene (from inks and markers on the stationery floor), and acetone (from the cyber café's electronics and cleaning products). Prolonged exposure at indoor concentrations causes headaches, eye irritation, and longer-term health effects. The indoor plants specified in Section 08a were selected specifically for their documented effectiveness at absorbing these specific VOCs from the air.
Marginal Plant
An aquatic plant that grows at the shallow margins of a pond — in water 0–30cm deep, with its roots submerged but its stems and leaves above the water surface. Marginal plants (Sacred Lotus, Indian Red Water Lily, Bulrush, Indian Iris) form the ecological transition zone between open water and dry land, providing habitat for invertebrates, spawning cover for fish, and shade that reduces pond water temperature. They are distinguished from floating plants (which have no soil anchor) and submerged oxygenators (which grow entirely below the water surface).
Riparian
Relating to or located on the banks of a river or other water body. A riparian species (such as the Arjuna tree, Terminalia arjuna) is one that evolved growing naturally at the edge of rivers and streams — it is therefore particularly well-adapted to environments with a nearby water table, periodic inundation, and high soil moisture near the water body. Riparian species are the correct choice for planting immediately adjacent to a pond, as they tolerate the soil conditions that kill other trees.
Deadheading
The practice of removing spent (finished) flowers from a plant before they set seed. Deadheading redirects the plant's energy from seed production back into flower production — extending the flowering season. For the Marigold bedding in the frontyard and along the path edge, monthly deadheading from October to March doubles the effective flowering period. For the Sacred Lotus in the pond, deadheading spent flowers in August encourages reflowering in September.
Fishless Cycling
The process of establishing the nitrogen cycle in a new pond without introducing fish. Pure ammonia solution is added to the empty (but filtered and pumped) pond water to feed the developing beneficial bacteria in the filter. The bacteria grow on the ammonia, converting it first to nitrite, then to nitrate. When ammonia and nitrite both read zero on a test kit and nitrate is detectable, the cycle is complete and fish can be introduced safely. The process takes 4–6 weeks. It is the single most important step in setting up the SCRIPTORIUM pond — skipping it or rushing it results in fish loss.
Weeding (landscape sense)
In a landscape context, weeding means the routine removal of unwanted volunteer plants (weeds) that compete with the chosen planting for water, light, and nutrients. Not to be confused with the library management term "weeding" (removing outdated books — Document 09, Section 07). In the SCRIPTORIUM garden, weeding is primarily required in the frontyard planting beds (where seeds blow in from the street) and around the pond's marginal shelf (where opportunistic aquatic plants can outcompete the chosen species). Monthly in the monsoon season, fortnightly in summer.
Construction & Materials
Dholpur Sandstone
A fine-grained, buff-pink to warm beige sandstone quarried in Dholpur district, Rajasthan. One of the most widely used building stones in North Indian heritage architecture — used in the Red Fort, Fatehpur Sikri, and much of Lutyens' Delhi. Specified for SCRIPTORIUM's building facade, frontyard boundary walls, and the campus signage stone. Not used in the pond. Dholpur is a porous sedimentary stone — permanent water contact allows mineral leaching that can shift pond pH and harm fish, and the stone itself stains and degrades when perpetually wet. The pond's coping and interior stone is granite, chosen specifically in a warm beige tone (Antique Beige Granite) to visually match the Dholpur palette while being chemically inert in water.
Kota Stone
A dark blue-grey limestone quarried near Ramganj Mandi, Kota district, Rajasthan. Widely used in Indian step-wells, courtyard paving, and commercial flooring. Not used in the SCRIPTORIUM pond — despite Kota stone's traditional association with water features and its apparent slip-resistance, it is a porous limestone and has been independently confirmed by multiple pond-construction sources as unsuitable for fish ponds: it can leach alkaline compounds into the water, gradually raising pH to levels stressful for Koi and Goldfish, and it flakes and stains over years of permanent submersion. The colour it was chosen to provide — the dark grey-green tone that matches the brand's "Kota Grey" palette swatch — is instead achieved using Steel Grey Granite, which is chemically inert and provides a near-identical dark cool-grey visual result in the water.
Granite (Pond specification)
An igneous rock formed by the slow cooling of magma, composed primarily of quartz, feldspar, and mica. Granite is chemically inert in water — it does not absorb water (non-porous), does not leach minerals, and does not alter pond pH regardless of how long it is submerged. This is why every authoritative pond-construction source specifies granite (alongside basalt and quartzite) as the safe stone choice for fish ponds, while flagging sedimentary stones like sandstone and limestone (including Kota stone) as problematic. SCRIPTORIUM's pond uses two granite varieties: Antique Beige Granite for the exterior coping (warm cream-and-brown tone, near-match to Dholpur sandstone's colour, preserving visual continuity with the building) and Steel Grey Granite from Karnataka for the interior floor, viewing ledge, and waterfall stacking surfaces (dark cool-grey tone, matching the brand's "Kota Grey" palette swatch, making fish maximally visible against a dark substrate). Both are widely available from Delhi NCR stone merchants — specify flame-finished or bush-hammered surface, never polished, on all wet-zone surfaces for slip safety.
Permeable Paving
Paving material or a paving system that allows water to pass through it into the soil below, rather than running off the surface to the storm drain. In the SCRIPTORIUM context, the outdoor café overflow zone and the strolling circuit path use permeable paving (sandstone sett pavers with open joints filled with fine gravel, or compacted gravel) rather than solid concrete or asphalt. Permeable paving contributes to groundwater recharge, reduces surface flooding during monsoon downpours, and keeps the outdoor zone cooler (water evaporating from the ground beneath the pavers has a mild cooling effect). Delhi building bye-laws increasingly mandate permeable surfaces in setback zones.
Coping Stone
The capstone that tops a wall, parapet, or in this case the edge of the pond. Coping stones protect the structure beneath from water ingress by throwing rainwater off the top surface. On the pond, the Dholpur sandstone coping slabs overlap the EPDM liner edge and overhang the water surface by 50–80mm — this overhang serves a practical purpose: it prevents wildlife from gaining a foothold to pull the liner upward, and prevents visitors from sitting on the edge (which would over time displace the coping).
Sett Paver
A small, roughly cuboid paving unit — traditionally granite or basalt, here specified in sandstone — laid in regular courses or random patterns to form a textured, durable paved surface. Sett pavers have open joints between them which makes them inherently permeable, easy to lift and relay if services need to be accessed underneath, and visually consistent with heritage and quality commercial environments. The term comes from the original Belgian sets (small granite blocks) used in European city streets.
Side Passage
A narrow corridor running along the side of a building, between the building wall and the plot boundary, connecting the frontyard to the backyard. Without a side passage, the boundary wall presses directly against the building's flank, leaving no security perimeter, no route for maintenance access, and no corridor for MEP service runs (water, drainage, electrical conduit) that need to travel from the street-side utility connections to the rear of the plot. SCRIPTORIUM specifies two side passages with different functions: the east passage as the primary service corridor (carrying MEP infrastructure, wider at 1.5–1.8m, taller boundary wall for security) and the west passage as a planted visitor corridor (narrower at 1.2–1.5m, lower wall, doubles as a secondary fire evacuation route).
MEP RoutingMechanical, Electrical & Plumbing Routing
The planned physical path that a building's service infrastructure — water supply pipes, drainage, electrical conduit, data cabling — takes from its source connection (the street, the DJB main, the electricity board transformer) to where it is used within the building and its grounds. In the SCRIPTORIUM landscape, MEP routing runs primarily through the east side passage: the DJB sump tank fill line, the pond inlet pipe, the rainwater downpipes, and the electrical supply to the pond pumps and waterfall all travel through this corridor. Planning MEP routing before construction (rather than retrofitting it later) avoids the cost and disruption of breaking into finished paving or walls to add services after the fact.
SmartFlowerGround-mounted biomimetic solar tracker
A ground-mounted all-in-one solar energy system whose 12 petal-shaped photovoltaic panels open, track the sun on a dual axis throughout the day, and close at sunset — mimicking the heliotropic behaviour of sunflowers. A cluster of 4–6 SmartFlowers is specified for SCRIPTORIUM's frontyard extension zone (Section 07a) as the campus's only on-site generation source — covering an estimated 11–27% of total electricity demand, not a majority share — replacing the flat rooftop solar panels originally specified in Document 02. Key advantages over flat panels in the SCRIPTORIUM context: (1) self-cleaning — integrated brushes sweep each panel clean on every open/close cycle, eliminating the daily water and staff cost of cleaning flat panels in Delhi's polluted air; (2) 40% higher energy output per nominal kW due to continuous sun tracking; (3) no roof penetrations — each unit installed on four earth screws in the ground with a single buried cable to the electrical panel; (4) no aesthetic conflict with the Gothic library tower — positioned in the frontyard as a cluster of sculptural objects rather than hidden behind a parapet; (5) automatic weather safety — petals retract at 47 km/h winds, fully close at 63 km/h (relevant to Delhi's Andhi season). The SmartFlower+ variant (specified here) adds an integrated battery for energy storage; SmartFlower also makes a base variant without battery and a SmartFlower EV variant with added EV charging, neither of which fits this brief. Produced by smartflower GmbH, an Austrian company (headquartered in Improving, Austria) — not a Boston-based product, correcting an earlier error in this entry. Importable to India; estimated price ₹15–22 lakh per unit, ₹60 lakh–₹1.32 crore for the full 4–6 unit cluster. As of 2026, no confirmed major commercial campus deployment of the SmartFlower has been documented in India at SCRIPTORIUM's scale — this would be a genuine first.
Extensive Green Roof
A shallow planted roof system (growing medium depth 60–200mm) designed for ecological function rather than human use — as opposed to an intensive green roof (growing medium 300mm+, designed for people to walk on and garden in). The SCRIPTORIUM rooftop pollinator garden is an extensive green roof: lightweight growing medium (expanded clay or pumice, not soil), planted with shallow-rooted perennial wildflowers and Sedum groundcover, accessible for maintenance but not for casual visitor use. Extensive green roofs require a structural live load of approximately 3.0–5.0 kN/m² (compared to a standard flat roof's 1.5–2.0 kN/m²) — this must be designed into the roof structure from the foundation. The ecological value is high relative to the floor area: an extensive wildflower green roof in an urban environment provides pollinator habitat, reduces urban heat island effect, improves roof waterproofing longevity by protecting the membrane from UV and thermal cycling, and creates a rainfall buffer that reduces peak runoff during monsoon events.
Dual-Axis Sun Tracking
A solar panel orientation system that adjusts the panel on two independent axes — horizontal (rotating east-to-west to follow the sun's path across the sky) and vertical (tilting upward or downward to maintain the optimal 90° angle to the sun as it rises and sets through the year). A fixed flat panel can only be set at one angle — optimal for one moment of the day and suboptimal for the rest. A dual-axis tracker like the SmartFlower maintains the optimal angle continuously, which is why it produces up to 40% more energy than a fixed panel of the same nominal capacity. In the SCRIPTORIUM context, dual-axis tracking also means the SmartFlower is always oriented toward the sun regardless of which direction it faces in the frontyard — placement flexibility that flat panels (which must face exactly south in the Northern Hemisphere for optimal performance) do not have.
Heliotropism
The biological tendency of a plant or organism to orient itself toward the sun — from the Greek helios (sun) and tropos (turning). The most familiar example is the sunflower, whose flower head tracks the sun from east to west each day. The SmartFlower's design is explicitly biomimetic — it mimics heliotropism mechanically, using sensors and motors instead of biological response. The name of the system, and the visual language of its petal-shaped panels, is a direct reference to this biological behaviour. For SCRIPTORIUM — a campus whose brand philosophy is rooted in the relationship between knowledge, craft, and nature — the SmartFlower's biomimetic design is not just technically convenient but conceptually consistent.
