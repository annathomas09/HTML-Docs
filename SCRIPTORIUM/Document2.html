Document 02 · Architecture Brief · Delhi NCT

The Study Campus — A Design Brief for Five Worlds Under One Roof

Gothic Cathedral · K-Drama Café · Japanese Capsule · Itoya Stationery · High-Spec Cyber Studio — unified by a central atrium and Delhi's own climate tradition

Founder: Anna Thomas Prepared: June 2026 Climate Zone: Delhi NCT — Composite/Hot-Dry Stage: Design Concept — Pre-Architect Brief

Premise The core design problem

You are asking five aesthetically incompatible worlds to share one address. Gothic cathedral grandeur sits next to Japanese white-pod minimalism; K-drama café warmth brushes against Itoya's gallery-retail precision; a dark, focused cyber studio lives down the corridor from all of them. Done badly, this is a theme park. Done well, it is a campus — a place where each zone has its own complete identity, but all of them belong to the same institution.

The solution is a unifying architectural wrapper and a central spine. The wrapper is the building's exterior shell and climate strategy — a single design language rooted in Delhi's own ventilation tradition. The spine is the central atrium corridor connecting all zones: a single, continuous, naturally lit passage that every room opens onto. Walk the spine and the building makes sense. Step into a zone and you've left Delhi entirely. Entry sequence: the library's Gothic portal — raised sandstone steps, heavy timber door, pendant lantern — is the primary campus entrance. Visitors pass through it first, enter the library (free, open, Gothic), and from there reach the atrium spine and all other zones through internal passages. The library is the arrival hall of the entire campus.

Every ventilation decision flows through the spine. Every material palette in each zone shares two common references with the others. The aesthetic jumps are real — but they are deliberate transitions, not accidents.

Shell The exterior — what Delhi sees from the street

The exterior is Neo-Collegiate Modernism with Climatic Vernacular Inflection. Think: Louis Kahn's IIM Ahmedabad in its discipline and material honesty, but lighter and more porous. The building must solve Delhi's climate before it solves its aesthetics — the best-looking interior in the world is worthless if it costs a fortune to cool in May.

The governing massing decision — made explicit in the revised elevation diagram above — is a split roofline. The library is a freestanding central tower with its own Gothic clerestory lantern roof, rising to approximately 22–26m. All three flanking wings (stationery, café+capsule stack, cyber café) share exactly the same flat roof height of 9m — creating a clean, uniform datum from which the tower rises with maximum contrast. No wing comes anywhere near the triforium level. Those flat wing roofs now carry an Extensive Green Roof pollinator garden — a shallow wildflower meadow, invisible from the street, screened by the colonnade parapet exactly as a solar array once would have been. The library's lantern peak is completely unobstructed, and the amber glow of the stained glass clerestory is the building's most visible signature from the street, especially at dusk. The library's height is never capped, never compromised.

Exterior Elements

  • Library tower as the dominant roofline feature — its Gothic clerestory lantern roof rises well above all other elements on the plot; this is the building's architectural crown and is never capped by the green roof or any other element
  • Split roofline — flat on the wings, Gothic tower on the library — the café, capsule, stationery tower, and cyber café wings all have flat roofs at a lower elevation; an Extensive Green Roof pollinator garden sits exclusively on these flat sections, with no rooftop solar hardware anywhere on the plot
  • Jaali screen facades on the east and west walls — perforated terracotta or stone screens reducing solar gain by up to 40% while providing passive ventilation and street identity
  • Deep overhangs (minimum 1.2m) on all sun-exposed faces — shade as infrastructure, not decoration
  • Ground-floor colonnade / verandah — a shaded walkway at street level providing transitional cooling before entry, and hiding the rooftop pollinator garden behind its parapet from the pedestrian's eye line, exactly as it once concealed the solar array

Exterior Materials

  • Agra red sandstone or Dholpur stone for the primary structural walls — warm, locally available, high thermal mass
  • Terracotta jaali panels — custom-cast perforated screens, a geometry inspired by Fatehpur Sikri's lattice work but in a contemporary repeat pattern
  • Weathering steel accents at door frames and canopy edges — the warm rust tone bridges the stone exterior and the dark Gothic interior steel inside
  • Low-maintenance native planting in the colonnade planters — Ashoka trees, Bougainvillea on the west jaali, Kaner (Oleander) in the courtyard

Zone A The Open Library — Gothic Collegiate

"The room that earns all the other rooms. Anyone who has stood in the Cathedral of Learning's Commons Room understands the physical sensation of being held by a space that believes in learning. Your library should produce exactly that — before a single book is touched."
Open Library Ref: Cathedral of Learning Commons Room, Pittsburgh (1937)

The Cathedral's Commons Room is 52 feet high, half an acre of Gothic stone, Indiana limestone walls, green Vermont slate floors, vaulted arches with no steel — pure Gothic structural logic. Your library cannot be 52 feet high. But it can borrow the three qualities that make the Commons Room feel timeless: vertical drama, material honesty, and controlled natural light from above.

Architecture & Interior

  • Double-height volume at ground/arcade level — minimum 6–8m floor-to-ceiling for the main reading hall itself, measured from the ground floor to the underside of the mezzanine gallery above. This is the room you actually stand in, and it is deliberately shorter than the tower's full exterior height — the two figures elsewhere in this brief (6–8m here, ~22–26m in the Shell and Massing sections) describe different things, not a contradiction. Above this double-height hall, the tower keeps rising through the triforium gallery and the clerestory (lancet gallery + pointed-arch rose window) to reach the full exterior height of approximately 22–26m at the lantern peak. This double-height base is non-negotiable; a single-height reading hall cannot produce the emotional effect you're after
  • Pointed arch motif in the shelving structure — not structural arches (expensive), but arched wooden shelf-top cutouts and arched doorway frames that echo Gothic language without the masonry budget
  • Rolling library ladders on brass tracks for top shelves — functional and deeply satisfying to look at
  • Three-level window hierarchy — the library interior reads exactly like the interior in the Gothic cathedral image you found: ground-floor arcade level (large pointed-arch openings flanking the portal), triforium level at the mezzanine height (a row of smaller arcaded windows lighting the gallery ring), and clerestory level in the section rising above all wing roofs (a continuous gallery of tall stained glass lancets, unbroken beneath the rose, with the rose window itself set into its own pointed-arch surround rather than floating alone)
  • Stained glass clerestory — the tall lancet windows in the clerestory section are fitted with amber and gold geometric tracery glass (clean Gothic patterns, not figurative scenes). The rose window at the very top of the clerestory section — now composed as a pointed arch over a continuous lancet gallery, with Rayonnant-style radiating tracery inside the rose itself — is the building's most visible signature from the street. This stained glass is exclusively in the section that rises above all wings — below it, all library windows are clear or amber-tinted. The stained glass does not disrupt the harmony of the other wings because it only appears at the height no other wing reaches.
  • Primary campus entrance — Gothic portal with raised steps — the library portal IS the main entrance to SCRIPTORIUM. Visitors approach through the colonnade, see the Gothic tower rising, and walk through the library's pointed-arch doorway first. Inside the library, passages lead to the atrium spine and all other zones. The portal itself is a deep recessed pointed arch (like Image 3 you found): heavy timber door with iron strap hinges, a pendant lantern in the arch, and 5 broad sandstone steps rising to the threshold
  • Clerestory windows running the tower's upper perimeter — high, north-facing for consistent indirect light, fitted with amber-tinted stained glass tracery that gives the warm golden quality described above
  • Mezzanine reading gallery on three sides, accessed by a wrought-iron spiral staircase — creates the ringing-the-room quality of the Commons Room's 3rd-floor classroom ring

Materials Palette

  • Walls: Dark sandstone plaster finish or actual Dholpur stone blocks — rough-hewn texture, not polished
  • Floor: Kadappa black limestone or Kota stone (grey-green) — the Delhi equivalent of the Commons Room's Vermont slate
  • Shelving: Dark walnut or teak — no white shelves in this room
  • Ceiling: Exposed dark timber beams across a plastered vault (not structural — decorative coffering applied to a concrete slab)
  • Lighting: Cast-iron pendant chandeliers above; individual reading lamps at every table are a curated departure from the Commons Room reference, not a copy — a curved gooseneck arm over a fluted/ribbed glass shade, dark bronze or black metalwork, each with a wireless charging pad built into the base. Warm 2700K throughout, lamps included. This is the one deliberate anachronism in the room: it reads as an antique fixture at a glance, but quietly solves the very modern problem of a student's phone battery during a long study session — a small, real point of difference from a library that copied the Cathedral of Learning wholesale
  • Reading lamp sourcing note: source this style (curved-arm, fluted-shade, wireless-charging table lamp) through a commercial lighting wholesaler or bulk furniture supplier rather than a small direct-to-consumer seller — a reading hall needs this fixture in quantity (one per table, so likely 40–60+ units), and that quantity needs consistent finish, a bulk quotation, and a real warranty/replacement channel behind it. Treat this the same way Document 08 (Sourcing & Procurement) treats every other bespoke fixture: get fabricator/wholesaler quotations before committing to a specific seller
  • Seating: Dark leather wing-back chairs, heavy oak study tables, no plastic anywhere in this room
🌬 Ventilation: Thermal stack via clerestory — hot air rises and exits through high-level operable vents. Floor-level fresh air enters through the jaali screens on the exterior wall. Large slow-ceiling fans (industrial aesthetic, dark finish) assist circulation. Backup: concealed ducted HVAC in the coffered ceiling cavities.

Zone B The Café — K-Drama Modern

Café / Catering Ref: Itaewon Class, Crash Landing On You café aesthetic — Seoul, present day

K-drama cafés have a very specific set of moves: floor-to-ceiling glass, exposed concrete, warm globe pendants, abundant indoor plants, clean lines, a visible kitchen, and the sense that the outside is always present inside. In your case, the "outside" that is always present is the backyard pond. The café is the hinge between the building and the landscape — the one zone that deliberately blurs indoors and outdoors.

Architecture & Interior

  • Full-height pivoting glass panels on the pond-facing wall — 3m tall panels that fold open in winter and cooler monsoon evenings, completely dissolving the café-garden boundary
  • Exposed concrete ceiling with raw texture (broom-finish or board-marked) — no paint, no suspended tiles
  • Open kitchen counter with a pass-through window facing the seating — customers see the coffee being made, exactly as in Korean café scenes
  • Step-up platform seating area near the glass wall — raised 20cm, with cushioned bench seating and the best view of the pond; a direct reference to K-drama step-up floor zones
  • Indoor plant wall on the back wall — a structured vertical garden with Pothos, Monstera, and Ferns, self-irrigated
  • Counter stools at a long bar along one window — single-person seating for solo studiers who want café energy but not conversation

Materials Palette

  • Floor: Terrazzo — white cement base with sage and terracotta aggregate, polished smooth. Delhi craftsmen are excellent at terrazzo; it's also cool underfoot in summer
  • Walls: Raw concrete on the ceiling side; a single pastel tile accent wall (matte sage or blush — choose one, not both)
  • Kitchen surfaces: White Carrara-look quartz counters, matte black tap fittings
  • Lighting: Globe pendants in warm amber glass (Edison bulb style), 2700K — no cool white in this room
  • Furniture: Mix of light oak and black metal — no matching sets, intentionally curated to look collected over time
  • Details: Brass hardware on every handle and hinge; these appear in almost every well-photographed Seoul café
🌬 Ventilation: Primary strategy is cross-ventilation — pond breeze enters through the glass panels (evaporative cooling from the water surface drops temperature 2–4°C before it even enters). Kitchen exhaust via a concealed hood above the cooking line, ducted to the roof. High-capacity cassette HVAC for Delhi peak summer (44–46°C days) — units recessed into the concrete ceiling, invisible from below.

Zone C Study Capsules — Japanese Pod

Private Study Capsules Ref: Nine Hours Capsule Hotel (Naruse Inokuma Architects, Osaka/Tokyo) + The Millennials Shibuya

Nine Hours is the gold standard here: four choreographed scenes — reception, prologue corridor, sanitary lounge, sleeping pod — each with its own precise color, material, and lighting. You adapt this sequence for study: reception → transitional corridor ("the quieting passage") → pod bay. The pod is not a hotel room; it's a larger study unit — 2m × 1.5m minimum — with a built-in desk, rented books on a small shelf, power, and a closing screen.

Architecture & Interior

  • The Quieting Passage — a 3–5m transitional corridor before entering the pod bay, ambient-lit in deep blue, acoustic panels on every wall; walking it signals the brain to shift from social to focus mode
  • Pod exterior: White fibreglass shells (or white-painted CNC-routed MDF for a budget version), flush-fitted doors with a single LED strip indicator (green = vacant, red = occupied)
  • Pod interior (minimum 2m × 1.5m): Built-in L-desk, built-in shallow book shelf (for 8–10 rented volumes), warm task light, USB-C + Type-B power strip, padded seat
  • Closing mechanism: Sliding frosted panel (not a hard door — provides visual privacy without the claustrophobia or fire-safety concerns of a lockable door)
  • Pod bay corridor: White floor, diffuse overhead lighting, numbered pod doors like hotel room numbers, clean monospace typography

Materials Palette

  • Pod shell: White fibreglass or high-gloss white lacquer on MDF frame
  • Desk surface: Pale birch plywood (warm contrast to the white shell)
  • Floor: Pale grey epoxy — seamless, easy to clean, reflects the ambient light
  • Acoustic panels in corridor: Dark charcoal felt — absorbs sound and visually anchors the transition from the social ground floor
  • Lighting: Warm white 2700K inside each pod (individual dimmer), cool 4000K in the corridor (focus-signalling contrast)
  • Typography: Laser-etched pod numbers, Helvetica or similar — the Japanese love for precision applied to wayfinding
🌬 Ventilation: Individual mini-split unit per pod bay (not per pod — one unit per 3–4 pods is workable). Fresh air via an Energy Recovery Ventilator (ERV) in the corridor mechanical chase — exhausts stale CO₂-heavy air and draws in fresh air without wasting cooled temperature. This is the critical system here; a sealed pod without fresh air supply becomes uncomfortable within 30–40 minutes. The ERV prevents this while keeping energy costs low.

Zone D Stationery — Itoya Gallery-Retail

Stationery — 3 Themed Floors Ref: G.Itoya Ginza, Tokyo — 12 themed selling floors, each a complete world

Itoya's entire identity rests on a single organisational move: each floor is a complete themed world, not a section of a bigger room. The act of going up a floor means entering somewhere different — a different sensory register, a different product universe, a different reason to stay. Their main building has 12 floors. Yours will have 3. But the logic is identical, and 3 floors done with Itoya's rigour will be more memorable than 12 floors done casually.

Shared Materials — All Three Floors

  • Walls throughout: Matte white — the only white rooms in the campus; the products are the colour; not a single coloured wall accent here
  • Shelving: Warm oak laminate on white MDF frames — prevents the white walls from feeling clinical
  • Floor (all 3 levels): Pale oak engineered wood — the same material on every floor creates continuity as you climb; you feel you are still in the same building despite the floor themes changing
  • Staircase: Open-tread oak stair with a brass handrail — visible from each floor's entrance, announcing there is more above; a deliberate invitation, not a utilitarian fire exit
  • Lighting: 3000K adjustable spotlight tracks on all floors — makes pen finishes, paper textures, and paint colours render at their best; never use cool white here
  • Signage: Category headers in black and gold, large and high-mounted; product labels in small monospace type; floor identity plaques at each landing
  • Try surfaces: Every floor has at least one table where products can be tested — this is the most important operational principle borrowed from Itoya, and the one most stationery shops skip
🌬 Ventilation: Each of the 3 floors needs its own ducted supply and return air — a single unit for the whole tower stack is not adequate. UV-filtering glass on all skylights and any south- or west-facing windows is non-negotiable; UV fades paper stock, ink packaging, and pen finishes within months of direct exposure. The top floor (Craft & Art) benefits from a north-facing UV-filtered skylight for even, diffused natural light — the best possible light for art supply selection and workshop use.

Zone E Cyber Café — High-Spec Creative Studio

Cyber Café Ref: Professional creative studio + premium gaming café — elevated, not garish

The most common mistake for a cyber café in this context: designing it as a dark gaming den, all RGB strips and neon signs. That works in a pure gaming centre but undermines your study campus identity. The right reference is a professional creative studio — high-spec equipment, controlled low lighting, acoustic isolation between stations, clean cable management, and a focused atmosphere that happens to also have excellent hardware. Think: the computer lab a design agency would build, not an esports arena.

Architecture & Interior

  • Station clusters of 4 separated by acoustic partition walls (1.5m high — sight isolation without full enclosure, which feels too prison-like)
  • Monitor arms at every station — no desktop monitor bases; this eliminates desk clutter and makes every surface feel clean and professional
  • Built-in cable management channels in each desk — no cables visible, ever
  • Standing desk option at 20% of stations — a signal of professional seriousness, popular with longer sessions
  • Acoustic ceiling tiles in a dark charcoal finish — absorbs both the ambient hum of equipment and keyboard noise between users
  • Server/equipment in basement — run structured cabling through conduits; keeping heat-generating equipment below keeps the room temperature manageable

Materials Palette

  • Desks: Dark matte surface (charcoal or black birch) — screens look their best against dark surroundings
  • Partitions: Dark acoustic felt panels in a grid frame — functional and good-looking
  • Floor: Dark grey epoxy or dark polished concrete — continuous and easy to maintain
  • Ambient lighting: Warm indirect LED coves at ceiling perimeter (2700K); focused cool task light at each monitor station (4000K, adjustable)
  • Accent: A single subtle under-desk LED strip per station in a neutral cool-white — not RGB rainbow, just a clean cool line that defines each workstation
  • Equipment spec: 27" IPS or OLED monitors, mechanical keyboards, ergonomic chairs — the hardware should justify the room
🌬 Ventilation: Computer equipment generates significant heat — 20 stations can add 5–7kW of heat load continuously. Design for hot-aisle/cold-aisle airflow: cold supply air delivered at desk level (below monitors), warm exhaust air extracted at ceiling. Separate precision cooling unit for this zone recommended. Server equipment in basement with its own dedicated cooling circuit. This is the zone where an MEP (Mechanical, Electrical, Plumbing) engineer should spec the system — don't estimate.

Zone F Public Washrooms — new zone, previously missing from this brief

Every other zone in this brief had a home; the washrooms didn't. Two aesthetic directions from your reference images, a specific smart-WC spec, the accessibility law that governs all of this, and a code-based fixture count — all below.

Two aesthetic directions (pick one per core, or one per core for variety)

  • Oxblood Classical — deep maroon/oxblood walls with fine-line Greco-Roman figure murals (etched or subtly embossed, not printed), a sculptural monolithic stone trough-basin on a fluted plinth, oval full-height mirrors rising into a coved ceiling, warm brass sconce lighting, and a graphic geometric floor (penny-round or circular tile in oxblood + cream). This is a strong match for the Gothic-collegiate identity elsewhere in the brief — same gravity, same sense of a considered, referential interior
  • Verdant Reflective — deep bottle-green fluted/ribbed wall panelling running floor to ceiling (including a rippled reflective ceiling finish), oval backlit mirrors recessed into the fluting, a dark stone or marble vanity counter with oval vessel basins, and warm pendant lighting with a sculptural glass shade. Reads more contemporary-luxury; a good fit if the washrooms should feel like a deliberate departure from the Gothic language rather than an extension of it
  • Either direction is genuinely "photographable" — both rely on a strong material (stone, fluted panel) plus a strong mirror shape plus warm, low, sconce-level lighting rather than flat overhead lighting. That combination, not any single element, is what makes a washroom Instagram-worthy — flat top-down lighting will flatten either direction into something ordinary

The smart WC — revised: Jaquar Bidspa (Electronic WC) replaces the Kohler Veil

  • Why the swap: Japan, China, and Korea popularised the high-tech-washroom category, but the technology itself — front/rear wash, heated seat, remote control, auto functions — no longer requires paying Kohler's price to get. Jaquar's dedicated smart-WC range, Bidspa, delivers the same hands-free, high-tech experience at a fraction of the Veil's cost, with the added advantage of Jaquar's existing India-wide dealer and service network — a real consideration for a fixture used by the public every day
  • Confirmed against Jaquar's own Bidspa product line: rimless bowl (no hidden rim crevices — genuinely easier to keep hygienic), remote-controlled dual wash with separate front and rear settings and adjustable pressure/temperature, integrated warm-air dryer on selected models, anti-bacterial seat and nozzle, sensor-based auto-flush with deodoriser, ambient night light, and — a genuine practical edge over the Veil in a public washroom — continues washing and flushing during a power cut. Ceramic backed by a 10-year warranty. Available in White, Black Matt, and Beige Matt; floor-mount or wall-hung
  • Price: ₹21,000–₹1,00,000 per unit depending on the specific Bidspa model (basic electronic seat-cover units at the low end; the full one-piece Bidspa Prime at the top), cross-checked against multiple Delhi-NCR and India-wide dealer listings. Treat this as an indicative range to confirm with an actual Jaquar dealer quotation — the same standard every other price in this brief is held to. Even at the top of that range, one unit costs roughly an eighth of the Kohler Veil; at the typical ₹21,000–₹42,000 band for a standard one-piece Bidspa, it's closer to a twentieth
  • What this changes about the old recommendation: at ₹7.9L a unit, fitting the Veil in one stall per core and conventional WCs elsewhere was the only sane move. At Bidspa pricing, that math flips — fitting all ~10 stalls across both cores costs roughly ₹2.1–10L total (vs. ≈₹79L for ten Kohler Veils). That's genuinely affordable inside the existing washroom budget, so "smart WC in every stall" is now a real option, not just the accessible-stall hero fixture. The signature-fixture-only approach is still fine if that saved money is better spent elsewhere — it's a choice now, not a necessity
  • If actual Japanese brand pedigree matters more than price for one hero stall: TOTO — the Japanese company that invented this category in 1980 — sells its Washlet line in India through an authorised Delhi dealer (Lajpat Nagar III), priced above Bidspa but still well under the Veil's one-piece price. A Washlet bidet seat can also retrofit onto a conventional WC pan rather than requiring a full fixture swap, which is worth asking the dealer about if only the accessible stall needs the "genuinely Japanese" story

Accessibility — this is law, not a style choice

  • Governed by the Harmonised Guidelines and Space Standards for Barrier-Free Built Environment (Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs, GoI), notified under Section 40 of the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016. Section 44 of that Act means a building plan that doesn't comply can be refused permission, and a completed building that doesn't comply can be refused an occupancy certificate — this is enforceable, not advisory
  • At least one unisex accessible washroom per floor, located adjacent to the accessible lift — this brief needs two (ground floor core + upper floor core), matching the two-core plan below
  • Door: 900mm clear width, opening outward. Wheelchair turning space within the cubicle: approx. 1500mm × 1500mm. WC pan projects ~750mm from the rear wall; seat height ~480mm from finished floor (vs. ~400–430mm for a standard fixture) — the specific Jaquar Bidspa model's exact seat height should be checked against this before it's specified for the accessible stall specifically
  • Grab bars, contrast-colour signage with Braille, and non-slip flooring are all part of the same mandate — treat this as a package, not an add-on gesture

How many fixtures — working from NBC 2016 ratios

  • NBC 2016 Part 9's public-assembly ratio: male 1 WC per 100 persons (up to 400); female 3 WC per 100 persons (up to 200) — the gap is deliberate; women take longer per visit on average, and modern codes now correct for it rather than splitting fixtures evenly
  • This brief has no fixed occupant-load figure yet (that's a proper fire/life-safety calculation your architect runs from final floor areas), so treat the following as a planning-stage placeholder: assuming ~150 people on-site at peak, split roughly evenly, gives ~2 male WC-equivalent (plus urinals, which aren't in the WC ratio above and are worth adding for practical male-facility throughput) and ~3 female WC
  • Recommended split — two cores, not one central block, and not one per pod: a ground-floor core (serving Library + Stationery-ground + Café + Cyber Café — the busiest, highest-footfall cluster) with roughly 2 male WC + 2 urinals + 3 female WC + 1 unisex accessible; and a smaller upper-floor core (serving Stationery FL1/FL2 + Study Capsule wing) with roughly 1 male WC + 1 urinal + 2 female WC + 1 unisex accessible
  • That's ~10 WC stalls + 3 urinals across 2 cores as a working number — recalculate once your architect fixes real seating/occupancy counts for the reading hall, café, and capsule wing; this NBC ratio is the formula to rerun, not a number to lock in yet

Connector The Central Atrium Spine

The spine is what keeps this from feeling like a mall. It is a double-height corridor running the full length of the building, with a skylight above that does three jobs simultaneously: floods the spine with natural light, creates a thermal chimney for passive ventilation, and gives every zone a shared visual reference. Walk down the spine: you see the Gothic arch of the library on your left, the greenery through the café glass ahead, the stationery's warm oak shelving to the right. You know you're in one place.

Spine Design Language

  • Material: Black Kota stone floor running the full length — a single continuous dark material that all the other floors branch from; stepping from the spine into a zone means stepping onto a different material (the transition is deliberate)
  • Ceiling: Double-height, with a continuous glass skylight panel above — north-facing to avoid direct sun while providing consistent daylight
  • Walls: Exposed red brick — the one material all Delhi architecture traditions share; connects the Gothic interior to the exterior sandstone and to the café's industrial concrete
  • Furniture: A single species of bench running the spine — dark steel frame, teak seat — for casual waiting or in-between moments
  • Basement access — confirmed off the spine, not a zone corner: the service stair down to the basement (storage / servers / mechanical) descends from the Central Atrium Spine itself, not from within any single zone. This is the correct call and worth stating explicitly: the spine is already the one route every zone opens onto, so a spine-based stair keeps the basement equally close for Reads, Ink, Studio, Capsule, and A&M Munchies staff, instead of quietly favouring whichever zone happens to sit nearest a corner stair. This corrects Document 10 (Landscape Planning), which currently places the basement stair in the building's north-west corner, tied specifically to the café's outdoor-furniture rotation — that reference should be updated to the spine location described here

The Ventilation Chimney Effect

  • The spine skylight creates a solar chimney — warm air rises through the double-height space and exits via operable skylight vents at the top
  • This passive exhaust draws fresh air in through the jaali screens on the building facade, the pond-facing café glass, and any open zone doorways
  • In winter and monsoon (when Delhi is actually pleasant), this system alone can maintain comfortable temperatures across most zones with zero mechanical energy
  • In peak summer (April–June, 44°C), the mechanical systems in each zone take over — but the chimney still reduces the cooling load by exhausting the hottest accumulated air

Climate Delhi-specific design adaptations

Site orientation — fixed now, while the plot is still unpurchased: street/frontyard faces south, backyard/pond faces north, the two flanking walls face east and west. This single decision is what makes every bullet below consistent with itself, and it resolves the SmartFlower shadow question raised in the Solar section further down. Treat "street frontage can reasonably face south" as a real site-selection criterion once you start viewing plots, not just a diagram convention.

The Pond as a Microclimate Anchor

  • A south-west-to-north-east oriented pond in the backyard intercepts Delhi's primary summer wind direction and pre-cools it before it enters the café
  • Water surface evaporation can reduce perceived air temperature by 2–4°C on dry summer days before the monsoon
  • Plant the pond edges with native shade trees — Arjuna (Terminalia arjuna) is both sacred and provides dense canopy; Water Hyacinth and Lotus on the water surface
  • The pond doubles as your rainwater storage basin (as established in the dossier) — design it with overflow into a recharge pit, not drainage
  • This pond axis is independent of the front/back orientation above — but worth a quick sanity-check with the architect: with the building mass now sitting between the street (south) and the pond (north), confirm Document 10's side passages actually give that south-west summer wind a clear path to the pond, rather than the building blocking it first

Sun Path & Shading

  • Delhi at 28.7°N keeps the sun in the southern half of the sky for almost the entire day, nearly all year — a south-facing frontyard sits on the sun's side for nearly all daylight hours, which is exactly why SmartFlower works there (see Solar section)
  • East and west faces get the worst afternoon and morning heat — these are exactly where the jaali screens go, on the building's two flanking side walls
  • South face — the street/entrance elevation — carries the colonnade as its deep overhang (1.2–1.5m): it blocks the low winter sun while excluding the high summer sun. This is the one overhang to prioritise if budget forces a choice, and this design already has it in the right place
  • The café glass panels face north toward the pond. Bonus from the orientation fix: the library tower's own afternoon shadow falls generally northward too — so it lands on the pond/café side, helping keep direct summer-afternoon sun off that glass wall rather than fighting it
  • The rose window is the one deliberate exception, not an oversight — it faces south, toward the street, specifically to be the visible signature, and its amber glass is chosen to turn strong light into a warm glow rather than glare. The "north-facing for consistent light" principle applies to the plainer clerestory lancets on the tower's other faces, not to the rose

Solar v3 SmartFlower — only on-site generation hardware, partial demand offset

Revised per Document 10 (Landscape Plan v6), and revised again here: flat rooftop solar panels are removed from this brief entirely — SmartFlower dual-axis trackers in the frontyard are now the campus's only on-site generation hardware, not a supplementary prestige piece alongside a flat-panel array. The flat wing roofs that solar once occupied are freed for the Extensive Green Roof pollinator garden below. But the honest number-crunching further down shows SmartFlower realistically covers a meaningful minority of total demand, not all of it — "sole source" was the wrong phrase, and it's corrected throughout this brief. The backyard remains fully committed to the koi/goldfish pond and cannot host any units — that, together with wanting more than one unit, is why the frontyard footprint needs to grow beyond its originally-planned 4–5m depth rather than compressing tree placement around one unit's clearance circle. That frontyard resizing is being finalised in Document 10; this brief now reflects the resulting placement decision so the architect sees one consistent story.

What a SmartFlower is

  • Confirmed against the official smartflower.com product line: three variants exist — base SmartFlower, SmartFlower+ (adds an integrated battery), and SmartFlower EV (adds direct EV charging). The + variant is the right call here, for the evening-battery reason below
  • An all-in-one, ground-mounted, dual-axis solar tracker shaped like a large sunflower — 12 petals unfold at sunrise and fold back at sunset or in strong wind, self-cleaning via brushes on the underside of each petal on every open/close cycle
  • Physical footprint: 5m tall when open, 4.8–5m petal diameter, 5.2m minimum clearance radius per unit — this clearance circle, multiplied across more than one unit, is why the frontyard needs the extra depth
  • Output: manufacturer-quoted 4,000–6,200+ kWh/year per unit depending on location, with dual-axis tracking delivering ~40% more energy than a fixed flat panel of the same rating — Delhi's high solar irradiance (~5.5–6.0 kWh/m²/day) works in the SmartFlower's favour
  • The SmartFlower+ integrated battery covers evening operating hours, since SCRIPTORIUM runs until 9pm
  • Originally an Austrian-engineered design (not a Boston product — flag this correction to Document 10 as well); estimated at ₹15–22 lakh per unit imported into Delhi NCR — confirm current pricing with a dealer before finalising the unit count and budget
  • Unit count: see the full demand-vs-supply calculation below — the short version is that a realistic frontyard can't fit enough units to cover the whole campus, so this is a meaningful partial offset, not full energy independence

Frontyard placement — shadow question resolved by fixing the site orientation

  • SmartFlower units were originally specified for the backyard in this brief specifically because the tower's afternoon shadow falls generally northward. Since the plot isn't purchased yet, the real fix isn't a shadow study on an unknown site — it's to choose the orientation so the frontyard is never on the shadow's side in the first place
  • Orientation now fixed: street/frontyard = south, backyard/pond = north (see the Climate section above). Delhi's latitude keeps the sun in the southern half of the sky for nearly all daylight hours, so a south-facing frontyard sits on the sun's side almost all day — the tower's shadow falls the other way, into its own backyard, not across the SmartFlowers
  • Side benefit: that same northward-falling afternoon shadow now lands on the pond/café side — exactly where the brief already wants shade, to keep direct summer-afternoon sun off the café's floor-to-ceiling glass. One orientation decision resolves both the SmartFlower shadow risk and the café glare problem
  • The only residual exposure is right at dawn and dusk, when the sun sits low and slightly north of due east/west and can throw a brief shadow with a southward component. Output at that sun angle is minimal for any solar installation regardless of shading, so it isn't a practical concern
  • When you view plots: treat "street frontage can reasonably face south" as a firm site-selection criterion, not a diagram convenience — it's what makes the colonnade overhang, the café glass, the jaali walls, and the SmartFlower placement all reinforce each other instead of fighting

Energy demand vs. SmartFlower supply — the honest math, and why "sole source" was wrong

No formal load schedule exists yet for SCRIPTORIUM — that's a real MEP calculation your engineer runs once floor areas are finalised. What follows is a planning-stage estimate built two independent ways, which converge closely enough to trust the range.

Estimated demand: ~140,000–200,000 kWh/year

  • Top-down check: India's Bureau of Energy Efficiency benchmarks commercial buildings at 200–400 kWh/m²/year typically, 180 kWh/m²/year for baseline ECBC compliance, and 100–150 kWh/m²/year for genuinely energy-conscious buildings. At an estimated ~1,100 sqm total built-up area across all zones (a first-pass figure from this brief's own zone descriptions, pending a real area schedule), a 130 kWh/m²/year "efficient, passive-design-forward" target (justified by the jaali, overhangs, ERV, and stack ventilation already in this brief) gives ~143,000 kWh/year; the 180 kWh/m²/year baseline gives ~198,000 kWh/year
  • Bottom-up check: summing rough per-zone estimates — including the Cyber Café's own stated 20 stations / 5–7kW continuous heat load from Zone E above, plus its dedicated cooling — lands at ~144,000 kWh/year, independently close to the top-down efficient scenario. That convergence is what makes the ~140,000–200,000 kWh/year range trustworthy as a planning figure
  • The Cyber Café alone is likely the single biggest concentrated load on the property — worth knowing before anyone assumes solar can casually cover "the whole campus"

Why 100% SmartFlower coverage doesn't fit the site

  • Delhi's strong irradiance pushes real-world SmartFlower output toward the top of the manufacturer's quoted 4,000–6,200+ kWh/year range — call it 5,500–6,200 kWh/year per unit here
  • Covering ~140,000–200,000 kWh/year at that output needs roughly 23 to 36 units
  • The footprint doesn't work: even at an efficient, non-overlapping clearance-circle packing (~85 sqm/unit), 23–36 units need ~1,950–3,060 sqm of frontyard — bigger than this brief's entire estimated building footprint. No realistic South Delhi frontyard holds that
  • Practical recommendation: 4–6 units in the enlarged frontyard (see below), covering roughly 11–27% of total demand — a genuine, meaningful offset plus the sustainability-identity and evening-battery value, with the grid (DISCOM connection) remaining the necessary backstop for the rest. This is normal for commercial solar in India, not a shortfall specific to this design

Extensive Green Roof — the flat wing roofs, freed from solar duty

With SmartFlower handling all generation from the frontyard, every flat wing roof (stationery, café+capsule stack, cyber café) is repurposed as a rooftop pollinator garden — private, not publicly accessible, and invisible from street level behind the same colonnade parapet that once concealed the panels. Full plant and irrigation specification lives in Document 10, Section 02a; the structural implication that must reach the architect and structural engineer immediately is below.

Structural load — must be designed in from the foundation

  • A standard flat roof in Indian commercial construction carries a live load of approximately 1.5–2.0 kN/m² — enough for occasional maintenance access, not for sustained planting
  • An extensive green roof (100–150mm lightweight growing medium, drainage layer, occasional pooled water) needs approximately 3.5–4.5 kN/m² — more than double the standard specification
  • This cannot be retrofitted. The enhanced load must be in the structural drawings — foundation, columns, and roof slab reinforcement — before construction begins, not added after a standard flat roof is already built
  • Flag this to the architect and structural engineer at the first design meeting, alongside the SmartFlower frontyard decision above — both are foundation-stage decisions, not finishing-stage ones

Why this replaces solar on every wing roof, not just some

  • The previous brief split solar duty: flat panels on all three wing roofs as the primary source, SmartFlower units in the backyard as a supplementary prestige piece. That hierarchy is now inverted — SmartFlower is the sole source, so no wing roof needs to reserve panel space
  • This also reframes the colonnade parapet's job — it now keeps the pollinator garden private and visually calm from the street, which was always its secondary function even when panels were up there
  • Net effect on the elevation and plan diagrams above: every "solar panel" annotation has been replaced with a green-roof / pollinator-garden annotation

Rules What holds this together — and what destroys it

Do

  • Let each zone's transition be an architectural moment — a change of floor material, a slight change of ceiling height, a door threshold — so visitors feel they have moved somewhere
  • Use dark walnut and brass as the two through-materials that appear in every zone in small doses — a brass handle in the stationery room, a walnut bench in the cyber café; this is the stitch
  • Light the library as one stop warmer than everything else (2700K vs 3000K elsewhere) — warmth reads as "ancient" at a neurological level
  • Commission a local artist for the library's mezzanine ironwork railings — the Cathedral of Learning's master blacksmith Samuel Yellin did exactly this, and those rails are the detail everyone photographs
  • Keep all signage in one typeface throughout the campus — choose Garamond or Caslon (classical/Gothic family) as the display font; this alone unifies more than any colour could
  • Specify acoustic ceilings in the capsule corridor and cyber café before anything else — the quality of silence is the quality of these zones

Do not

  • Never put RGB lighting in the cyber café — it signals gaming den and undercuts the study-campus identity you're building
  • Never mix floor materials within a single zone — the floor is the zone's identity; tile in one corner of the library and it stops being Gothic and starts being a college canteen
  • Don't use white walls in the library — not a single white surface should exist in that room; it will look like a hospital the moment sunlight hits it
  • Don't put natural light in the cyber café — screens are unreadable in direct daylight; controlled artificial light is the correct choice and is an authentic feature of the reference aesthetic
  • Don't let the stationery zone become a stockroom — Itoya displays 12 items where a normal shop would display 60; edit your inventory ruthlessly and give each product breathing room
  • Never cut the mezzanine from the library budget — if the library must be single-height, it loses the Cathedral of Learning quality entirely; the mezzanine is structural to the concept, not decorative
