00 How to read this document — start here

This document contains financial projections for SCRIPTORIUM, the study campus planned for Said-ul-Ajaib, South Delhi. It is written for Anna as the founder, not for an accountant or a bank manager. If you have never read a business financial model before, this section explains every term used throughout the document in plain language, with examples. Read this section before reading anything else.

The one question this entire document answers: Can SCRIPTORIUM pay its own way? Specifically — will the money coming in from visitors (capsule fees, café sales, stationery, cyber café, events) eventually be enough to cover the money going out (staff wages, electricity, bank loan repayments, maintenance) — and if so, when? Everything in this document is in service of answering that question as honestly as possible.

The numbers you will see throughout — what they mean

All figures in this document are in Indian Rupees. Two abbreviations are used constantly:

₹1 Lakh (L) = ₹1,00,000 (one hundred thousand rupees). When you see "₹5.5 L/month," that means five and a half lakh rupees per month — ₹5,50,000.

₹1 Crore (cr) = ₹1,00,00,000 (one hundred lakh = ten million rupees). When you see "₹10 crore," that means ten crore rupees — ₹10,00,00,000.

Glossary — every financial term used in this document

Revenue
The total money coming in to the business from customers. If 10 people book a capsule at ₹3,500/month each, the capsule revenue that month is ₹35,000. Revenue is counted before any costs are deducted. It is not profit — it is just the top line of money arriving.
Revenue Stream
A separate way of earning money. SCRIPTORIUM has five: study capsule memberships, café sales, stationery retail, cyber café fees, and events/workshops. Each stream earns money independently. If one is slow, the others can compensate — which is exactly why having five matters.
COGS — Cost of Goods Sold
The direct cost of what you sold, and nothing else. For the café: the cost of the coffee beans, milk, and food that went into what the customer bought. For stationery: the wholesale price of the pens and notebooks sold that day. Staff wages and electricity are NOT in COGS — those go under Operating Expenses.
Gross Profit & Gross Margin
Gross Profit = Revenue minus COGS. It is what is left after paying for the thing you sold, before paying for anything else. Gross Margin % is that number expressed as a percentage of revenue. SCRIPTORIUM's blended gross margin is ~74%, meaning out of every ₹100 earned, ₹74 remains after the direct cost of goods.
Operating Expenses (OpEx)
All the costs of running the business day to day that are not directly tied to a specific sale: staff wages, electricity, internet, insurance, CA/accountant fees, marketing, maintenance. These costs exist whether you sell one capsule membership or a hundred that month.
EBITDA
Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation, and Amortisation. Think of it as what the business actually keeps after paying everyone who works for it and everything it uses to run — but before paying the bank or the government. It is calculated as: Revenue minus COGS minus OpEx. It is very different from Revenue: Revenue is all money arriving; EBITDA is what survives after staff wages, electricity, marketing, and maintenance have all been paid. EBITDA positive = campus operations are self-sustaining. EBITDA negative = the campus costs more to run day-to-day than it earns, and cash must be injected from outside. Example: if SCRIPTORIUM earns ₹20L in a month and pays ₹18L in staff + utilities + maintenance, EBITDA = ₹2L.
EBIT
Earnings Before Interest and Tax. This is EBITDA minus Depreciation. Because Depreciation is a non-cash accounting charge (see Depreciation above), EBIT is mostly used for tax calculations and for comparing SCRIPTORIUM's performance against other businesses in the same industry. It is less useful for day-to-day management than EBITDA, but a bank or investor will often look at EBIT when evaluating the business because it accounts for the write-down of assets over time.
PAT — Profit After Tax
The final bottom line of the P&L — the number at the very end of the table after every single cost (COGS, OpEx, Depreciation, loan interest, and income tax) has been subtracted from revenue. In this model, PAT is negative in all 5 years due to high depreciation and loan interest charges. A negative PAT does NOT mean SCRIPTORIUM is losing cash from operations — it means the accounting treatment of the building's cost makes the P&L look like a loss on paper. The real cash test is always EBITDA vs. EMI, not PAT. PAT becomes positive once depreciation charges reduce in later years and the loan balance reduces.
Footfall
The number of people physically entering the campus in a given period — daily, weekly, or monthly. Footfall is the engine that drives all five revenue streams: more people walking in means more capsule bookings, more café purchases, more stationery sales, more cyber café sessions, and more workshop sign-ups. SCRIPTORIUM Reads (the free open library) is the primary footfall driver — it brings people through the door who did not necessarily intend to spend money, and a portion of them convert to paying customers across the other zones. This is why the library being free is a business decision, not just a social one. Monitoring weekly footfall vs. revenue conversion rate per zone is one of the most important operational habits for the campus manager.
Depreciation
A legal accounting method of spreading the cost of a big purchase over many years. If you spend ₹8 crore fitting out the campus, accounting rules say you cannot count all ₹8 crore as a cost in Year 1 — you must spread it over 10 years (₹80 lakh/year). This ₹80 lakh annual depreciation is not extra money leaving your account — the cash already left when you paid for the fit-out. It is a paper number, not a real cash outflow.
Finance Cost / Interest
The interest portion of your bank loan repayments. When you borrow ₹10 crore at 10% per year, you pay ₹1 crore in interest in Year 1. This IS real cash leaving your account — unlike depreciation. The interest portion shrinks over time as the loan principal reduces.
Profit Before Tax (PBT)
What is left after subtracting everything — COGS, OpEx, depreciation, and interest — from revenue. In this model, PBT is negative in all five years. This does not mean the business is failing. It means depreciation (a paper cost) and loan interest (a real cost) are together larger than EBITDA in the early years. This is completely normal for a ₹20+ crore property-backed project.
EMI
Equated Monthly Instalment — the fixed monthly bank loan repayment. For a ₹10 crore loan at 10% over 20 years, the EMI is approximately ₹9.65 lakh every month, without exception. Each EMI payment contains both interest (reducing every month) and principal repayment (increasing every month).
Break-Even
The monthly revenue level at which income exactly equals costs. Below break-even, the business loses money every month and you need to inject cash to keep it running. Above break-even, it sustains itself. SCRIPTORIUM has two break-even points: the operating break-even (covering day-to-day costs, reached around Month 22–25) and the debt-service break-even (covering day-to-day costs plus the full EMI, reached around Year 6–7).
Working Capital
The cash buffer you need before the business starts paying its own way. Think of it as petrol in the tank before the engine starts earning. SCRIPTORIUM needs approximately ₹1.1 crore–₹1.4 crore in working capital to cover staff, utilities, and marketing during the first 6–8 months before revenues reach operating break-even.
GMV (Gross Merchandise Value)
Used only for the stationery stream. GMV is the total value of everything sold at retail price before deducting the cost of those goods. If you sell ₹6.5 lakh of pens and notebooks in a month, the GMV is ₹6.5 lakh. The gross profit after buying that stock (at ~55% of GMV) is approximately ₹2.9 lakh.
GST — Goods and Services Tax
A tax the government requires you to collect from customers on most sales, and pass on to the government. You are not paying this tax yourself — you are collecting it on the government's behalf. Example: a customer pays ₹180 for a coffee. If GST is 5%, then ₹8.57 of that ₹180 belongs to the government. You keep ₹171.43. At the end of the month, you send the government all the GST you collected. GST rates differ by what you sell — café food is 5%, pens are 18%, notebooks are 0%.
ITC — Input Tax Credit
The right to get back the GST you paid when buying things for your business, by deducting it from the GST you collected from customers. Example: SCRIPTORIUM buys a gaming monitor for ₹30,000 + 18% GST = ₹35,400 total paid. With ITC, the ₹5,400 GST paid on that purchase is a credit — deducted from the GST collected from cyber café customers. Net GST payment to government = GST collected minus ₹5,400. The café does NOT get ITC — the government's rule for standalone restaurants specifically blocks Input Tax Credit. This means every rupee of GST paid when buying café equipment, gas, cleaning supplies, or ingredients is a permanent cost that cannot be recovered. This is why café pricing must be calculated more carefully than the other zones.
LAP / CGTMSE / Udyam
LAP = Loan Against Property — a bank loan where your land or building is the security/guarantee. CGTMSE = a government scheme that guarantees MSME loans so banks will lend without extra collateral, up to ₹5 crore. Udyam Registration = the free government registration that makes you an official MSME and unlocks access to these preferential loan rates. Without Udyam, you pay commercial rates (14%+) instead of MSME rates (8.8–12%).
Occupancy Rate
For the study capsule wing specifically: what percentage of the 20 pods are booked at any given time. 40% occupancy = 8 pods out of 20 booked. The model projects 40% in Year 1, rising to 78% in Year 3. Most well-run paid study libraries in Delhi operate above 80% occupancy once established.

The three numbers to keep in your head

Everything else in this document is detail. These three numbers are the ones that determine whether SCRIPTORIUM works:

₹14.4 lakh/month — the operating break-even. This is the monthly revenue level at which the campus covers all its day-to-day costs (staff, utilities, maintenance, marketing) from its own income. The model shows SCRIPTORIUM crossing this line around Month 22–25. Below this number, you are injecting cash every month. Above it, the campus runs itself.

₹9.65 lakh/month — the bank loan EMI. This is the fixed monthly repayment on the ₹10 crore construction loan, every month for 20 years, regardless of how the business is performing. It does not pause during a slow month. Everything in the financial model is built around the question of when EBITDA is large enough to cover this number on top of operating costs.

Year 3 — when positive EBITDA begins covering a meaningful portion of the EMI gap. By Year 3, the campus generates ₹37.0 lakh in EBITDA annually, is fully operationally self-sustaining, and the gap between EBITDA and the EMI is being funded by a reducing working capital reserve rather than fresh cash injection. From Year 3 onward, the project is on a clear trajectory to full debt-service self-sufficiency by Year 6–7.

Why Profit Before Tax is negative in all 5 years — and why that is fine

The 5-year P&L table shows negative Profit Before Tax (PBT) in every year. For someone reading a financial model for the first time, this looks alarming. It is not. Here is what is actually happening:

The fit-out costs ₹8 crore. Accounting rules say this must be spread over 10 years as depreciation — ₹80 lakh per year of paper cost on the P&L, even though the cash already left your account when the work was done. Add ₹98 lakh of loan interest in Year 1. Together, depreciation plus interest = ₹178 lakh of charges against the P&L in Year 1 alone. EBITDA in Year 1 is negative ₹35 lakh. So PBT = −35 − 178 = −₹213 lakh on paper.

The real test is EBITDA vs. EMI. EBITDA is the real cash the operations generate. EMI is the real cash the bank collects. In Year 3, EBITDA = +₹37 lakh. EMI = ₹115.8 lakh. Gap = ₹78.8 lakh that year, funded from reserves. By Year 6–7, EBITDA at continued 12% growth covers the EMI entirely. At that point, the property is also worth significantly more than it was in 2026.

In short: the business is healthy from Year 2 onward on an operating basis. The negative PBT is an accounting artefact of depreciation, not evidence of failure.

01 Capital structure — how the ₹22–28 crore is funded

A project of this scale in India is typically financed across three channels. The exact split depends on whether the land is already held by family (which dramatically reduces the loan quantum) and on the promoter's CIBIL score and existing collateral.

Tranche A — Promoter Equity
₹7–10 crore
30–40% of total project cost. Can include family land value (if already owned), personal savings, and family contribution. If the Said-ul-Ajaib plot is already held by family, this tranche effectively absorbs the entire land cost (₹4–7 cr) and the capital requirement reduces significantly. A Private Limited Company is the recommended entity — it separates personal liability from business liability, is required for bank financing at this scale, and allows clean equity transfer if a co-investor joins later.
Tranche B — Secured Construction Loan / LAP
₹10–14 crore
Loan Against Property (LAP) or commercial construction loan, secured against the plot. SBI's MSME loan rates start at 8.80% p.a.; HDFC starts at 10.75% p.a. For a Udyam-registered Pvt. Ltd. company, SBI or a PSU bank (PNB, Bank of Baroda) is typically the most cost-effective lender. SIDBI direct lending starts from 7.82% for priority-sector MSME projects. Realistic rate for a first-time commercial borrower with clear title and a project report: 9.5–11% p.a., tenure 15–20 years. EMI on ₹10 crore at 10% for 20 years: approximately ₹9.65 lakh/month.
Tranche C — MSME Working Capital / OD Facility
₹2.5–3.5 crore
Overdraft facility or short-term MSME loan for equipment, inventory, and the ramp-up loss buffer. Drawn down in tranches as needed — not all at once. Interest rate: 11–13% p.a. under CGTMSE scheme (Government-backed, no collateral required on this tranche up to ₹5 crore). MUDRA's Tarun category covers up to ₹10 lakh at preferential rates for the first small tranche. Working capital requirement has been revised upward to ₹1.1 cr–₹1.4 cr to reflect the extended ramp-up period (Month 22–25 break-even). Register on Udyam portal before applying — Udyam Registration is required for all MSME scheme access and is free.

If the land is already family-owned: Total external borrowing drops to approximately ₹12–16 crore (construction + fit-out + equipment + working capital). At 10% for 20 years, the LAP on ₹10 crore = ₹9.65L/month EMI. This is the number everything else in this model must be built around — your business must generate enough cash to cover this EMI, operating costs, and still leave a margin for reinvestment and owner salary.

03 GST treatment — every revenue stream, correctly

Getting GST wrong is the most common and most expensive early mistake for multi-service businesses. The campus has five different GST treatments across its revenue streams, all updated for GST 2.0 effective September 22, 2025. Appoint a CA before opening — the mixed-rate stationery inventory alone requires careful HSN-code-level classification.

Open Library
0% / Nil
Free service — no GST applicable. No revenue generated, so no GST obligation. Donated books: exempt. Optional library card donations: below ₹40L annual threshold, likely exempt; check with CA once turnover is determined.
Café — Food & Beverages
5% (no ITC)
Standalone café/restaurant: 5% GST (2.5% CGST + 2.5% SGST). No Input Tax Credit allowed — GST paid on kitchen equipment, supplies, utilities is a sunk cost. You cannot reclaim it. Price your menu knowing 5% is passed to customer and not recoverable on inputs. Packaged bottled water sold from café: 18% GST (separate HSN). Alcohol: state excise, not GST (requires separate licence).
Study Capsules (rental/subscription)
18% (with ITC)
Service — rental/subscription for private accommodation space. HSN 9963 (accommodation services) or 997212 (licensing of intellectual property, depending on how the booking is structured). 18% GST applies. ITC is claimable on pod construction inputs, HVAC, ERV, and furnishings related to the capsule wing — this is a meaningful advantage vs. the café. Rented books component: price separately as a library rental; check ITC chain with CA.
Stationery Retail
0% / 5% / 18% mixed
Most complex stream. Post-GST 2.0 (Sept 22, 2025): Nil (0%): Notebooks, exercise books, pencils, crayons, erasers, sharpeners, atlases, printed maps. 5%: Geometry sets, colour/maths boxes. 18%: Pens (all types — ballpoint, gel, fountain; HSN 9608), highlighters, markers, staplers, paper clips, art supplies, inks, wax seals. You can claim ITC on all stationery purchases (unlike the café). This reduces your effective input cost meaningfully on the 18%-GST items.
Cyber Café (internet/IT services)
18% (with ITC)
Internet/IT service provider: 18% GST on charges to users (HSN 998314 — internet access services). ITC claimable on hardware, server equipment, networking gear, and software licences. Membership packages: same 18% rate. Hourly access fees: 18% GST. This is the highest-margin zone for ITC recovery — the hardware investment (₹18–26L) generates significant ITC in Year 1.
Events / Workshop / Space Hire
18% (with ITC)
Event space rental and workshop fees: 18% GST (SAC 997212 or 9963 depending on format). ITC claimable on event-related inputs. If workshop is run by an educational institution with formal recognition, some services may attract exemptions — consult CA on specific workshop structure before pricing.

Critical: The café's 5% no-ITC rule is the most important single GST fact in this business. Every rupee of GST you pay on café-related supplies — kitchen equipment, gas cylinders, cleaning materials, even a portion of rent if it's on a GST invoice — cannot be recovered. Budget your café margins accordingly. A well-run standalone café in India effectively carries a 3–5% unrecoverable tax drag on inputs. This is why the capsule wing (18% with ITC) and cyber café (18% with ITC) are your strategically stronger revenue streams from a tax perspective, even if the café is the visible face of the campus.

05 5-year profit & loss projection

All figures in ₹ Lakhs. Revenue is shown net of GST collected (i.e., the amount that is yours — not the tax you pass to the government). COGS includes raw material, direct stationery inventory cost, and rented book procurement only. Gross Profit = Revenue – COGS. Operating Expenses covers staff, utilities, maintenance, marketing, insurance, and CA/legal fees. EBITDA = Gross Profit – OpEx. Depreciation is calculated per Income Tax Act schedules. EBIT = EBITDA – Depreciation. Finance costs = EMI interest component only (principal repayment is balance sheet, not P&L). PBT = EBIT – Finance Cost. PAT = PBT × (1 – 22% tax rate), applied only when PBT is positive.

How to read this table — row by row

Revenue rows (at the top): Money coming in from each source — capsules, café, stationery sales, cyber café, events. These grow each year as more people discover SCRIPTORIUM.

COGS rows: The direct cost of what was sold — café ingredients, stationery stock bought at wholesale. Not staff or electricity — just the goods themselves.

Gross Profit: Revenue minus COGS. At ~74% gross margin, for every ₹100 earned, ₹74 remains after the cost of goods. The other ₹26 went to buying the café supplies and stationery stock.

OpEx rows: All running costs — staff wages, electricity, internet, marketing, insurance, CA fees, licences. These are fairly fixed regardless of how busy the campus is in any given month.

EBITDA: The most important line. Negative in Year 1 (the campus costs more to run than it earns). Turns slightly positive in Year 2. Grows strongly from Year 3 onward. This is the real cash position of the operations before the bank is paid.

Depreciation rows: These are paper costs only — not cash leaving the account. They make PBT look worse than the cash reality. See Section 00 for the full explanation.

Finance cost rows: The interest on the bank loans — this IS real cash leaving the account each month as part of the EMI.

PBT (Profit Before Tax): Negative in all 5 years due to depreciation and interest. This is expected and is explained fully in Section 00. Focus on EBITDA instead for the true picture of how the business is performing.

Net Cash Position: EBITDA minus the total annual EMI. Shows how much additional cash needs to be drawn from the working capital reserve each year. The gap narrows every year and closes around Year 6–7.

Sequencing matters: Register on the Udyam portal before approaching any bank — every MSME scheme (CGTMSE, Mudra, SBI SME) requires an Udyam Registration Certificate. 09 Key risks & mitigations

High
Construction overrun — cost and time
The most common killer of projects like this. A 6-month delay burns ₹55–60L in working capital with zero revenue. Mitigations: fixed-price contract with the main contractor (not cost-plus), a 10% contingency budget built into Phase 1 costs, and a penalty clause for delays beyond agreed milestones. Appoint a Project Management Consultant (PMC) alongside the architect — their fee (typically 1.5–2% of construction cost) pays for itself in the first delay they prevent.
High
Lal dora title defect on land
If the title is challenged after construction has begun, the entire project stalls. Non-negotiable mitigation: commission a title search from a property-specialised Delhi lawyer before any payment to the seller. Verify that the plot is not sub-judice, that all mutation entries are clear, and that the seller's chain of title goes back at least 30 years. Budget ₹50,000–1 lakh for this legal due diligence — it is the cheapest insurance you will ever buy.
Medium
Revenue ramp slower than projected
If Year 1 revenue is ₹70L instead of ₹105.8L, the working capital gap widens by ₹35L. Mitigations: begin marketing 3–4 months before opening (social media presence, targeted outreach to UPSC aspirants, DU students, and readers along the Saket metro belt); offer 3-month discounted founding memberships for study capsules before the building opens; partner with a coaching institute for group booking of capsule bays in the first year. Pre-sell at least 10 pod memberships before opening day.
Medium
Interest rate increase on floating-rate LAP
SBI's MSME rates are RLLR-linked and move with RBI repo rate decisions. A 150 bps rate increase on ₹10 crore raises annual interest cost by ₹15L. Mitigation: model your finances with an additional 150 bps buffer (i.e., assume 11.5% rather than 10%). Alternatively, negotiate a fixed rate for the first 3–5 years with your lender when rates are relatively low.
Medium
GST misclassification penalties
A multi-stream business with 0%, 5%, and 18% GST treatments across stationery items alone is a compliance trap. A GST notice for wrong classification can result in back-tax demands with 18% interest and penalties. Mitigation: appoint a CA with GST specialisation (not just a general accountant) from day one. Set up HSN-code-level billing software (Busy, Zoho Books, or Tally Prime) before the first sale. Run a GST compliance audit at the end of Year 1.
Lower
Cyber café hardware obsolescence
Gaming/creative hardware depreciates rapidly — an RTX 4070-tier rig that is premium in 2026 will feel mid-range by 2028. Budget ₹5–8L for a hardware refresh fund from Year 3 onwards, funded from cyber café operating surplus. Structure the cyber café fit-out so that workstation desks are modular — hardware can be upgraded without replacing the entire desk unit.
Lower
Key staff attrition (especially head barista and campus manager)
The café's identity and the capsule wing's daily operations both depend heavily on 2–3 key people. Build retention into their compensation from Year 2: a profit-linked bonus (5–8% of the zone's EBITDA) is inexpensive when margins are good and powerfully retentive. Document all systems and recipes so that attrition does not mean institutional knowledge walks out the door.

10 What this model needs before a bank will look at it

A bank lending ₹10+ crore will require a Detailed Project Report (DPR) — a formal document combining this financial model, the architecture brief, the cost estimation, the regulatory compliance record, and a market study. The DPR is what this model becomes after you have: (a) a sanctioned building plan from an architect, (b) three quotations from construction contractors, (c) a formal site valuation from an approved valuer, and (d) a CA-certified balance sheet for the promoter entity. Most PSU banks have a standard DPR format on their SME lending portal — download it early and build toward it.
