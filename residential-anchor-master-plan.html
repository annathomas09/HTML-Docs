Residential
Anchor.

The first and primary business — the ground everyone else stands on. Not a housing project attached to a university, but the living place the rest of the ecosystem gets built on top of: rental homes for anyone, rent-free homes for every person who works across the businesses, and gated blocks for TAU's own guests and students, all on one shared architectural language. The ambition underneath it is larger than one plot — this is meant to be a working demonstration of what Delhi could look like if any of it were built this way: fewer lanes given to cars, more to trees, water, and people, proven on land this project actually owns rather than argued for on land it doesn't.

Governing Principles

What already governs this, applied to housing specifically

Nothing here is new philosophy — it's the Code of Architectural Aesthetic and the first-principles framework from Basics of Building Cities, translated into what they actually mean for a residential block.

01 — Mass

Climate-responsive brick

Natural brick and lime plaster, load-bearing where possible. No glass-and-steel curtain walls — thermal mass over air conditioning as the default.

02 — Grid

Walkable, human-scaled

Blocks sized for a five-minute crossing on foot, not superblocks. Every ground floor is a frontage, never a wall.

03 — Density

Integrated, not adjacent

Mixed income in the same stair core, not a market-rate tower next to an EWS block. Mixed use on the ground floor of every tier.

04 — Water

Green-blue infrastructure

Wetlands and permaculture farms are structure, not landscaping — sized to recharge the water table, not decorate a lobby.

05 — Identity

Distinct, shares DNA

Brick is this anchor's own material signature — related to the University's limestone and the Board's granite, but never mistaken for either.

06 — Privacy

Unity without exposure

Shared courtyards and sightlines that build community, with jali-screened openings that keep every individual unit its own sanctuary.

Space Standards

How much space, per person — not just per unit

DDA's density ceiling says how many people the plot can hold. It says nothing about whether they'll have anywhere to breathe once they're there — that's a separate national standard, and this plan now measures against it explicitly rather than assuming good intentions cover it.

URDPFI GUIDELINES, 2014 · MINISTRY OF HOUSING & URBAN AFFAIRS — OPEN SPACE NORMSSOURCE: MoHUA URBAN GREENING GUIDELINES
BenchmarkStandard
Recommended open space (URDPFI)10–12 sq.m per person, plain areas
Community open space, alt. metric1.2–1.4 hectares per 1,000 population
WHO minimum benchmark9 sq.m per person
EU / UN reference points26 sq.m (EU) · 30 sq.m (UN) — aspirational, not adopted in India
URDPFI Green City provision25–35% of total developed area as recreational + open space
This standard is meant to be met city-wide — dedicated park land elsewhere in the zonal plan, not a requirement on any one private plot. This plan self-provides anyway, on the assumption that a Delhi zonal plan already stretched thin on paper (most Indian metros run at a fraction of their own URDPFI target — Mumbai near 1–2 sq.m per person, Chennai under 1) won't reliably deliver a park within walking distance of every unit here.
Worked example — Why ground-plane parks can't carry this alone
DDA ceiling: 900 persons/hectare (Group Housing max)
URDPFI target: 10–12 sq.m open space per person
900 persons × 10–12 sq.m  =  9,000–10,800 sq.m required, per hectare
Total land available, per hectare  =  10,000 sq.m — buildings, roads, everything included.
Rule: at DDA's own maximum density, the full URDPFI benchmark cannot be met from ground-plane space alone — there isn't enough hectare left over once the buildings that house those 900 people are standing on it. This is exactly why this plan counts the rooftop pollinator garden and the podium permaculture farm as real open space, not decoration: stacking green space vertically is the only arithmetic that closes at this density, not a stylistic preference.

Status

MPD-2021 is still the operative law

Delhi has been governed by the plan notified in 2007 (the "2021" plan) for its entire building-control envelope — including every figure in the Design Law table above. The Master Plan for Delhi-2041 has been in draft since June 2021 and remains unnotified; the housing ministry said in June 2026 that notification is in its final stages, but that has been said before. Anything drafted against MPD-2041 provisions right now is drafting against a document that isn't law yet.

Transit

Yellow Line today, two more corridors converging by ~2029

Saket station isn't just nearby — it sits directly on Mehrauli-Badarpur Road in Said-ul-Ajaib itself, per DMRC's own station record; Malviya Nagar and Qutab Minar are a stop or two further along the same Yellow Line. Phase 4 adds two more corridors to this immediate area, not one: the Golden Line (Aerocity–Tughlakabad) routes through Chhatarpur, Neb Sarai, and Saket G-Block — Neb Sarai sits right next to this site — and the separate Brown Line (Lajpat Nagar–Saket G Block, 8.4 km, 7 stations) terminates at that same Saket G-Block interchange. Both are under construction, targeted around 2029. None of that matters if the walk there is bad. This project's own prior research on Indian metro ridership found commuters abandon the metro for private vehicles once the home-to-station trip passes twenty minutes — mostly from broken footpaths and inconsistent last-mile options, not from the metro itself. The fix isn't a better metro. It's a better 800 m around it — which is what the streets section below is actually for.

Opportunity

TOD policy — notified while this plan was in progress

Delhi's Transit-Oriented Development policy was formally notified in April 2026 — after the draft version already sitting in this project's own reference folder. It allows FAR up to 400 (500 with additional charges) on plots of 2,000 sq.m+ that fall fully or at least half within a 500 m band of a Metro corridor, against 200+15% under standard Group Housing norms. Participation is voluntary, and comes with a real condition attached — reportedly around two-thirds of that FAR has to go to units under 100 sq.m, worth confirming against the primary DDA notification rather than this summary.

Worked example — What TOD eligibility would actually change
Standard Group Housing ceiling → FAR 200, +15% EWS bonus over and above → effective ≈ 230
TOD base, on payment of TOD charges → FAR 400
TOD maximum, additional FAR charges to DDA → FAR 500
Rule: if a survey confirms this plot sits inside the 500 m Yellow Line band, TOD roughly doubles buildable area before a single extra square metre of land is bought — against TOD charges, a compact-unit-mix condition, and DDA/UTTIPEC co-authoring an "Influence Zone Plan" for the node before any scheme is sanctioned. That last part is the one place in this entire document where opting in trades away "no government involvement" for a lot more buildable area. Worth deciding on purpose, not by default.
Fig. 2 — Feeder shuttle loop, campus roads only Not the public Delhi BRT (dismantled 2016 after eight years fighting mixed traffic) — a private loop with no lane to lose to anyone

Worth saying plainly: Delhi does not currently have a functioning BRT network to map. The Ambedkar Nagar–Moolchand corridor ran from 2008 to 2016 and was dismantled after years of fighting general traffic for the same lanes — the same fate has since met Bhopal's BRT, and partial dismantling is underway in Indore and Jaipur. Maximum Comfort

What "comfort" actually means in Delhi's climate

Not amenities bolted on at the end — comfort as a function of mass, shade, airflow, and acoustics, worked out at the same time as the regulatory envelope above.

Thermal mass over air conditioning

Climate

Load-bearing brick and breathable lime plaster hold the day's heat out and release it slowly at night — the default cooling strategy, not a backup to mechanical systems.

Jali screens, doing two jobs at once

Climate + Privacy

Perforated brick or terracotta screens on west and south faces cut direct sun and street sightlines simultaneously, while still allowing cross-ventilation through the gaps.

Shaded courtyards, not open lawns

Public Realm

An exposed lawn is unusable for half the year here. Deep-shaded, tree-canopied courtyards are the real outdoor comfort space, and the actual site for community life.

Stack-effect ventilation through the atria

Climate

The FAR-free double-height atria aren't just legal upside — vented at the top, they pull hot air up and out of the stair cores and corridors below.

Acoustic buffering by adjacency

Privacy

Party walls detailed for sound isolation, with bedroom-to-bedroom adjacency between units avoided in the floor plan wherever the block geometry allows it.

Universal Design as baseline, not retrofit

Compliance + Comfort

Step-free routes, lift access on every residential core, wider door clearances — DDA-mandated, and treated here as comfort infrastructure for elderly and differently-abled residents, not a checkbox.

Security & Access

A gradient, not a fortress

"Rental houses for everyone" and "dedicated separate gated society blocks" both appear in the same paragraph of the Relationship document — the way to honor both at once is to make gating a property of Tier 3 specifically, not a wall around the whole site.

TIER 1 — OPEN

No gates. Safety comes from active ground-floor frontage, street lighting, and natural surveillance — eyes on the street, not a checkpoint.

TIER 2 — CONTROLLED ENTRY

Standard residential access, keyed to employment. Same visual language as Tier 1, no perimeter distinct from the surrounding blocks.

TIER 3 — GATED

Controlled entry, CCTV-covered pathways, direct-hire security with crowd-management training — the same staffing model already established for SCRIPTORIUM.

The bioswale and draining kerb, at street scale

The rain-garden edge and the perforated draining kerb are the same technique DUAC catalogues for Delhi under Bio-filtration Swales — already running in Indian conditions, not an imported concept; Pune has documented examples in daily use.

  • Soil mix: max. 5% clay content, engineered to filter
  • Rainfall handling: 120–250 mm/hour throughput
  • Continuous slope: 4:1 ideal, 3:1 maximum
  • Placement: multi-utility zones, medians, cul-de-sacs — anywhere outside the walkway itself
  • Kerb detail: perforated precast kerb units, draining laterally into the swale bed, not a sealed storm drain
  • Pairs directly with: the podium rainwater harvesting and constructed wetland specified below — street runoff feeds the same water table, not a separate system
Block Pattern

A loose grid, run as one superblock

The cross-section above governs what one street looks like. It says nothing about how streets connect to each other — that's a separate layer, and it was genuinely missing. This plot works as a single superblock: perimeter road carries anything that needs to move fast or far, and the entire interior is one connected, loosely-gridded, pedestrian-priority zone — not a rigid Manhattan grid, and not a suburban cul-de-sac either.

Does It Actually Work

An honest audit, not a sales pitch

Five direct questions, answered plainly — including where the answer is "partly," because a document that only ever says yes isn't auditing anything.

Pedestrian & cycling friendly?

Yes, inside the gate — not guaranteed outside it

Every internal street defaults to the multimodal section above: continuous 1.8m+ footpath, 2.0–2.5m cycle track, on every road, not just a showcase corridor. The honest limit: the moment anyone crosses this plot's boundary onto the public 18m ROW road toward the metro, they're on infrastructure this plan doesn't control. If that stretch has no footpath, that's a real gap this document can't close by itself.

Fulfils the needs of people?

Across tiers, not just for the ones paying market rate

Gym, Urban Forest, cross-campus library and café access, houseplants at handover, universal design already specified under Comfort — all three tiers, not an amenity list that quietly excludes Tier 2 staff housing. Still open: specific eldercare and childcare provision hasn't been scoped yet, and it should be before the architect locks the unit mix.

Supports MPD & transit expansion?

Built ahead of the curve, not caught behind it

TOD eligibility is already scoped as a live decision, not an afterthought. Both the Golden Line and the Brown Line are priced into the transit picture years before either opens. Cycle tracks and footpaths here already clear UTTIPEC's current minimums, which means if MPD-2041's eventual NMT provisions land stricter than MPD-2021's — the draft signals they will, though nothing is confirmed until notification — this plan isn't the one that needs retrofitting.

A genuinely public-centric area?

Tier 1's frontage does real work here

A&M Munchies and SCRIPTORIUM Reads are walk-in, not resident-gated — that's what makes Tier 1's "active frontage instead of a checkpoint" security model function at all. Genuinely open: whether the Urban Forest's paths stay resident-only or open to the public on posted hours once its 3-year establishment window closes. That's not decided yet, and it should be a deliberate choice, not a default.

Signals built for people walking?

Mostly moot — there's almost nothing to signal

A shared, walking-speed interior with no cut-through traffic doesn't need signals; that's the point of the superblock pattern above, not a gap in it. The one place a signal genuinely matters is the gate interface with the public road, where this plan would specify a leading pedestrian interval, walking-speed-based clearance timing, and an audible accessible signal — global best practice, recommended here, not a claim that this plan controls a public intersection it doesn't.

Ecology, Not Just Landscaping

The Urban Forest — a third green layer, distinct from the farms

Two different green systems were getting folded into one language in the last version of this plan, and they do two different jobs. The podium permaculture farm grows food for the people living here. The Urban Forest doesn't — it exists so a piece of Delhi's own displaced ecosystem has somewhere on this plot to come back to, and the wetland lives inside it now, not next to it.

METHOD
Miyawaki dense native afforestation — multi-layered, native-species-only planting at roughly 3–4 saplings per sq.m, first proven in India near Bangalore in 2009 and now standard practice across Indian cities for exactly this kind of pocket-forest.
FUNCTION
Habitat corridor + wetland host + groundwater recharge + heat-island mitigation. Not food production, not a lawn — a working ecosystem, distinct from the podium permaculture farm below and from the rooftop pollinator garden above.
PLACEMENT
Ground-plane pockets at each block's low point — the same location the constructed wetland already needed — plus any leftover ground area not claimed by courtyard or circulation. The wetland sits inside the forest footprint, sharing its canopy and root system rather than standing alone in an open bed.
RESULT
Documented biodiversity 18–20× higher than a conventional managed plantation of the same area in published Miyawaki-method studies — birds, pollinators, and amphibians the wetland alone wouldn't support. Self-sustaining within roughly three years; near-zero maintenance after establishment, unlike a lawn or a farmed bed.
DISCIPLINE
Sited only on already-cleared or built footprint within the plot — never a justification for clearing existing mature tree cover elsewhere on site. That's the one real critique of Miyawaki projects elsewhere in India, and it doesn't apply here as long as this rule holds.

Every rooftop carries native flowering species tuned for continuous bloom succession, not food crops — the same principle already running at SCRIPTORIUM's green roof, now made consistent across the ecosystem instead of drifting into a second permaculture farm.

Houseplants, every unit

Standard, not upsell

A planted balcony or windowsill allowance is specified into every unit at handover across all three tiers — indoor greening as a baseline finish, not a market-rate-only amenity.

Solar-ready roofs

Forward link

Structural and electrical provisioning reserved now on every rooftop so the future Green Energy business can integrate without a retrofit — panels elevated above the pollinator bed rather than replacing it, the same "biosolar" combination already common on European green roofs.

High-End Amenities

What "premium" means beyond the unit itself

Amenities follow the same rule as the housing tiers above: quality doesn't get rationed by which tier is paying market rate. What's built once gets used by all three.

Fitness & Wellness Centre

Cross-business

Podium-level gym open to residents across all three tiers, not gated to market-rate units — staffed and programmed by the Medical & Wellness business on the same partnership-not-extension terms it already holds with TAU, rather than run as a separate concierge add-on.

The Urban Forest, as amenity

Dual purpose

Everything specified under Ecology above does ecological work first — but the same paths that make it a habitat corridor make it a walking and birdwatching amenity for residents, distinct from a manicured lawn and worth counting on this list too, not only the ecology one.

Cross-campus access

Already built in

SCRIPTORIUM Reads' open library and A&M Munchies' embedded café sit on or beside this site by design — residents get both without either business building a second copy of something that already exists a courtyard away.

Below Grade

World-class subterranean infrastructure — water first

Delhi's own street design guidance names the problem plainly: uncoordinated utility trenching, the same stretch of road cut open again and again for lack of a shared duct to work from. Nothing about that is inevitable — it's a design choice this plan doesn't have to repeat on its own land. This runs deeper than the last version: water first, because that's where the real design decision sits, then the rest of what this project's own subterranean research folder actually scopes for at residential scale.

Water — why this doesn't need a rooftop tank at all

Countries without rooftop tanks don't have them because the tap runs straight off a continuously pressurised main — there's nothing to buffer, because the pipe itself never runs dry. India's own housing ministry defines the goal in exactly those terms: a distribution network "continuously full and under positive pressure," per the CPHEEO manual. The reason most Indian buildings carry a rooftop tank at all is that municipal supply is intermittent, not that storage has to live on the roof.

PROVEN INDIAN PRECEDENT — STORAGE MOVED UNDERGROUND, NOT ELIMINATEDSOURCE: CPHEEO MANUAL · COIMBATORE & KARNATAKA 24×7 PILOTS · CHANDIGARH MANIMAJRA
PrecedentWhat it proved
Hubli-Dharwad, Belgaum, Gulbarga (Karnataka, 2003–)Demonstration zones ran full 24×7 continuous supply straight to the tap — no household tank, rooftop or otherwise, needed at all
Coimbatore 24×7 projectExplicitly asked residents to disconnect their underground sumps, not just rooftop tanks — direct connection held 7–25m of pressure at the consumer end
Chandigarh, Manimajra (2024)Two underground service reservoirs plus a pressurised main deliver supply to the 4th floor with no per-building storage of any kind
DJB doesn't run 24×7 supply to Said-ul-Ajaib today, so this plan can't assume continuous mains pressure the way Manimajra can. The response: one large underground service reservoir sized to the occupant-based worked example earlier, plus a booster pump system feeding pressurised risers to every unit — the same buffering job a rooftop tank would have done, just relocated below grade. The roof stops being infrastructure and goes back to being the pollinator garden, full stop, not a compromise between the two.

SystemDesign rule
Common utility ductOne continuous corridor for water, sewerage, stormwater piping, power, fibre & gas — routed under the multi-utility zone, not the carriageway, so a repair never means cutting open a finished street
Potable water supplyUnderground service reservoir + booster pumps, pressure-zoned by tier — see above, not a rooftop tank
Sanitary sewerFully separated from stormwater at every point — the sewer only ever carries wastewater to treatment, never diluted street runoff
Stormwater & rechargeRouted to the street bioswales and the Urban Forest's wetland, not the sanitary line — the two systems never meet underground

The guiding rule, straight from DUAC's own guidance: utilities should not run below the carriageway "unless there is an acute lack of space," and should be placed so each one can be individually serviced and repaired without touching the rest.
Beyond water — the rest of the project's own subterranean scope

The project's own Subterranean Facilities research folder scopes considerably wider than one housing plot needs. Here's what actually applies at this scale, pulled straight from that folder's own category names.

WASTE
Pneumatic waste collection (AVAC-type) — sealed inlets at each block feed an underground vacuum network to one collection point, so no truck or bin room sits on any pedestrian-priority street; already running at Indian scale in Amravati's capital-city pilots
LOGISTICS
A single underground service tunnel handles deliveries into A&M Munchies and SCRIPTORIUM Ink's back-of-house — the same principle large campuses and hospitals already use to keep delivery trucks off streets this plan just spent two sections making pedestrian-priority
DIGITAL
A basement network-operations room — campus wifi, CCTV, smart metering, and the fibre backbone terminate here. Not a hyperscale data centre, just enough to run the estate's own systems without renting rack space off-site
COOLING
District cooling is the one item on this list Delhi's climate actually calls for over district heating — a shared chilled-water loop off one efficient central plant instead of a condenser unit hanging off every window, the same approach GIFT City has already run at Indian scale
PEDESTRIAN LINKS
Basement-level covered links between podium levels across Tier 2 and Tier 3, for monsoon and peak-heat months specifically — not a full underground city, just enough to keep the "no car needed" promise honest in July and August too
What's deliberately not here

The same research folder also scopes Deep-Earth Scientific Research Laboratories, Global Preservation & Cultural Vaults, Strategic Storage & Civil Defense, and Subterranean Highway Tunnels. None of them belong in a housing master plan, and forcing them in here would be padding, not planning — a highway tunnel is government-scale infrastructure no private developer builds unilaterally, and deep-earth labs or cultural vaults read as a different venture's problem, most plausibly TAU's research campus, not the Residential Anchor's. The one piece worth keeping at residential scale: a modest emergency-preparedness room per block — supply storage and a designated shelter space for flood or heat-emergency events, sized like any large residential complex already provides, not a civil-defense bunker.

Execution

Building this without government involvement — what's actually true

Not every piece of this plan is equally free of government contact. Most of it is entirely this plan's own design choice; a few pieces still need a routine sign-off. A sign-off is not the same thing as the government designing these streets instead of this plan — here's the honest split, item by item.

01

Draw the internal layout as private estate roads

Private design

Everything inside the plot boundary — courtyard streets, cycle tracks, bioswale edges, the shared slow-speed carriageway in the streets diagram — is drawn by the project architect as part of the layout plan. There's no separate road-design authority reviewing internal circulation on a private Group Housing plot the way UTTIPEC reviews a public arterial; the architect designs it, DDA checks it against the Design Law bye-laws, not against a competing design of its own.

02

Keep the parking mandate off the surface

Private design

The 2.0 ECS/100 sq.m parking requirement doesn't specify where those spaces sit — podium and basement parking, already planned as FAR-free upside, satisfies it exactly as well as surface parking would. Meeting the parking norm and building a car-light surface street aren't in tension; they're two different floors of the same building.

03

Size the fire route first, then design everything else around it

Routine sign-off

Delhi Fire Service requires a minimum clear width — typically 6 m — and turning radius on at least one route to every block, under the National Building Code. It's the one non-negotiable geometry in the street diagram above, but it's a checklist item on the building-plan application, not a separate planning process run by someone else.

04

Write the internal traffic rules into the estate's own management framework

Private design

Speed limits, where private cars stop at the gate versus where they're allowed to park, shuttle and e-rickshaw access for Tier 3 — none of it needs government approval, because it isn't public traffic law. It's the same kind of internal rule an RWA or a university campus already sets for its own roads; IIT Delhi runs on exactly this basis today.

05

Specify the bioswale, draining kerb, and utility duct as civil-drawing line items

Private design

Permeable kerb units and bio-filtration swale beds are a materials and detailing choice for the civil engineer's drawings, sourced from any precast supplier building to the DUAC spec — not a request needing a separate environmental permission. If anything, it strengthens the rainwater-harvesting compliance already mandatory under the Building Bye-Laws rather than adding a new approval to chase.

06

Get the water systems rubber-stamped, not redesigned

Routine sign-off

Rainwater harvesting is mandatory regardless of street design; DJB/CGWA review the harvesting capacity and recharge method, not whether it starts at a building downspout or a street bioswale. Extending it from podium level to street and subterranean level doesn't change what gets approved — only how much of the site it covers.

07

Decide TOD deliberately, not by default

Routine sign-off

The one place in this whole document where opting in means DDA/UTTIPEC co-authoring an Influence Zone Plan for the node — real government involvement, in exchange for the FAR upside in the worked example earlier. Every other item on this list stays entirely this plan's own design, start to finish.
